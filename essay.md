# (I) An overview of AI anxiety

The early weeks of 2026 have been filled with intense public discussion about new and emerging risks developing as a result of powerful, rapidly advancing AI, especially in domains like national security and macroeconomic health. Many others have already written about these risks broadly and in detail—below I've shared a list of links to materials that I used in my research. Suffice it to say that the opportunity to upset the status quo, along with significant uncertainty about who the winners and losers will ultimately be, has people at all levels both excited and worried about the future.

Nowhere has this been on greater display than in worker and consumer confidence, which has been continuing its downward trend despite easing inflation, especially among higher-income earners. Well-publicized layoffs and increasing rates of long-term unemployment are now signs of a supposed "AI jobs apocalypse," and highly-educated people fear that AI will quickly solidify into a "general cognitive labor substitute," making it economically impossible to perform brainy work for a living ever again.

I'll go ahead and spoil the ending here by saying that I think this outcome is exceedingly unlikely, at least on a timescale that matters to people (we can use 40 years as a reference point for the average lifetime career). But I am by no means an AI skeptic, a viewpoint that becomes more untenable with each passing day. This technology is obviously transformational, and my optimism is based on the assumption that we, as a society, want desirable outcomes during and after the disruptions AI will bring, that we will be motivated to chart a clear path forward, and that we will be successful in following that path and course-correcting responsively.

During my "sabbatical" I have continued to work on personal software projects, recently getting derailed by exploring AI coding tools, being wildly impressed, and falling into the rabbit hole of online essays and analysis. _The commentary so far has mostly been driven by intelligent laypeople making fuzzy, subjective, and sometimes sensationalistic claims; policy non-experts expressing opinions on policy; and technology non-experts expressing opinions on technology._ Obviously I'll be adding to this mismatch in some ways, but I think I can provide a fresh perspective as someone with serious professional technical expertise and a strong interest in economic policy and STS (my college minor). Building on this analogy, it's very important to recognize that _we do have some control over the process_, but we also need to know _how and why_ to exert that control. Just as it's critical to know where the steering wheel is, it's also critical to wipe the windshield and turn on the headlamps. All I'm trying to do here is make sure we can see where the bus is heading so we know when to actually turn the wheel.


# (III) The outcome that pessimists fear

A "dark AI" future is one filled with rampant threats to physical security and widespread economic subjugation. In this world, AI is broadly and completely powerful. Human cognition is inferior in all situations, and perfectly operating robots handle all manual labor. Control over critical pieces of AI infrastructure, like data centers, is centralized at the very top of society, and AI is only accessible to average people for trivial uses, or in ways that enhance societal control. Power struggles at the top and rogue-actor terrorism mean there are ever-present risks from cyberweapons, bioweapons, or worse. Average humans are so worthless that there's no official organization that prioritizes protecting individual lives. We have failed to overcome the three major challenges in a world without labor scarcity, as posed by Messrs. Autor and Thompson: life and community have no meaning, all resource allocation decisions are made unilaterally by the Altman-Musk-Zuckerberg commission, and all democratic governments have fallen and been replaced by technofeudalist states.

This really is the stuff of dystopian science-fiction nightmares. But perhaps that's why apocalyptic projections about AI have gone viral in the first place—dystopian science fiction is, for some reason, quite a popular genre of fiction (emphasis on _fiction_). I do think that the dark AI outcome logically follows if all of these premises hold true: AI will advance far more rapidly than we can handle institutionally (destroying all white-collar jobs), AI-enhanced physical automation will mature shortly thereafter (destroying all blue-collar jobs), and we don't successfully design a just and sustainable system for resource distribution as the old capitalist wage-labor system inevitably breaks down. But we have good reasons to expect that perhaps none of these premises are true, and many different strategies we can use to try to stave off their worst effects if they turn out to have a path to becoming reality.


# (IV) The physical world imposes limits

The core user experience of AI is a digital, "disembodied" one. Sure, you need to use some kind of computer interface, but those are easily exchanged and replaced—nothing happens to the AI if you lose your phone or laptop. Because the actual physical machinery of AI is hidden by the internet (and all its wireless connections and miles of buried or undersea cables), it's easy to misunderstand what AI actually needs to work, much like how someone who played The Sims too much can be astonished at how complicated it is to build a real house—it can take 18+ months even if you're infinitely rich!

Land, electricity, GPUs, and GPU components (like memory chips) are all indispensible inputs for AI, and right now they are all in short supply (yes, including land, which is available only through the goodwill of local communities). Any new production is capital-intensive and will take time to ramp up. This is why people are seriously floating the idea of putting data centers in space. Clearly this is an obstacle to AI everything, everywhere, all at once. Consumer-facing prices still look reasonable because, just as in the early days of Uber, they are heavily subsidized by investors. Masa is willing to burn even more on AI than the (inflation-adjusted) $100+ billion he lost during the dot-com crash, and that's one guy.

Outside of the economic costs of simply expanding the functionality we already have, using AI to press ahead with innovation in important physical domains like robotics and biotech will be far slower than progress in the AI models themselves (a purely digital endeavor). The physical world always runs at 1x speed, which drags out testing and iteration cycles. Even Mr. Amodei has written that AI may be "limited by the response time of the physical world or of software it interacts with." Providing some concrete examples will really drive this key point home. If AI has produced genetically modified crop seeds, we still have to wait for them to grow just like any other plant. Robots have to be manufactured and assembled before we can test how well their AI software works. Few people would be willing to take a new AI-designed pill before animal trials have been run. AI absolutely can greatly accelerate the pace of progress in these fields, but not to the point where a single small team can drop new finalized discoveries every month like AI labs releasing new model versions. I am reminded of a classic maxim often repeated in the software industry: nine women can't make a baby in one month.

Regarding security, AI will increase the accessibility of engaging in terrorism and other nefarious activity. But being able to successfully execute a terrorist attack isn't just a knowledge problem; it's also a physical one. Let's say I want to build a nuke. I can't just ask ChatGPT to deliver a pile of uranium to my front door, no matter how well I understand the physics of bomb-building. Sure, it's a ridiculous example, but it illustrates a reasonable way to approach the issue. What if I wanted nitrogen fertilizer instead? As with uranium, the government exerts some control over that [^B1 [link](https://chatgpt.com/share/69a51084-314c-8013-a96a-2a160a7c7d74#:~:text=strict%20security%20oversight%20on%20ammonium%2Dnitrate%20fertilizer)]. Maybe this is the type of "heavy-handed" economic disruption that Mr. Amodei wants to avoid, but society seems to accept it. The need is clearly understood, though loss of life was sadly required to get us there.

One of the reasons why those who worry about AI safety focus so much on bioterrorism and cybersecurity is that these attack strategies have minimal physical material requirements. But even here, I think fortune favors the good guys, if we can adopt the right defenses. Restrictions similar to those applied to fertilizer can also be applied to biotech inputs, like centrifuges which are readily available on Amazon, or gene synthesis companies which are happy to print out dangerous biological agents [^A15 [link](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1hNUnU8i2yubt5uesmmV17aTJXhYYDgTY)]. Yes it's unpleasant, but remember COVID? As for cyberattacks, Mr. Amodei has already made the key observation: the offense-defense profile is significantly more balanced, and we'll be in good shape as long as we provide generous support (including AI) to white-hat and purple-teaming cybersecurity researchers.


# (V) Discovery is not advancement: extrapolation to infinity is unlikely

New technology has always been accompanied by optimism about its potential—optimism that often ends up looking hopelessly naive in hindsight. Bitcoin, or other blockchain tokens more generally, was supposed to help solve world hunger, poverty, and corruption [^B2 [link](https://chatgpt.com/share/69a63890-6928-8013-b1bc-cf99eaa1c640#:~:text=narrative%20held%20that%20blockchain%20technology%20could%20%E2%80%9Csolve%E2%80%9D%20structural%20global%20problems)]. We are well past 50 years overdue for "real" self-driving cars, based on the earliest predictions for when they would be available [^B3 [link](https://chatgpt.com/share/69a63871-6538-8013-b639-13a69aa071d3#:~:text=predictions%20about%20widespread%20self%2Ddriving%20cars%20appeared%20surprisingly%20early)]. This pattern extends beyond technology; for example, many of the earliest uses for crude oil were as misguided medicinal treatments for skin, respiratory, and digestive issues [^B4 [link](https://chatgpt.com/share/69a640f7-2cf0-8013-9dea-ea3f23ec2d13#:~:text=petroleum%20products%20that%20were%20widely%20promoted%20at%20the%20time%20but%20later%20proved%20exaggerated%2C%20incorrect%2C%20or%20outright%20harmful)]. I think OpenAI's decision to present their LLM research as a chatbot was a stroke of product genius, because chatbots have enthralled people since the early days of ELIZA despite obvious limitations [^A16 [link](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ELIZA_effect)]. Remove those obvious limitations and you get the fastest-growing consumer application in history, and the wild claims to match.

One big pillar supporting the belief in AI's unstoppable path to world domination is AI's "scaling laws," often illustrated with a now-famous METR graph drawing an exponentially increasing curve [^A17 [link](https://metr.org/blog/2025-03-19-measuring-ai-ability-to-complete-long-tasks)]. (The graph is usually shown as a straight line but with a logarithmic y-axis.) As The New Yorker's recent profile of Anthropic [^A18 [link](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2026/02/16/what-is-claude-anthropic-doesnt-know-either)] tells it, Mr. Amodei and some close colleagues were important players in prioritizing model size (input and parameter count) over "fancy contraptions," and proven right when simply "scaling" size well past what other teams thought reasonable led to something robust enough to power ChatGPT. That might explain why, on a psychological level, he still places such faith in scaling, saying he has "increasing confidence in the trajectory of AI and the likelihood that it will surpass human ability across the board."

LLM-based AI is quite unlike past technologies, however. We're "growing" these models rather than designing them. What if AI's improving capabilities are being "found"—discovered—rather than created? A claim like "more hardware and inputs and parameters means more model capability" could actually map to something like "more pumpjacks and wells means more oil." The knowledge, information, and connections are all already there in the training data, generated by humans with human limitations, and the training process "extracts" a model, much like how all the oil is already there in an oil field, and the extraction hardware pulls it out. Adding more hardware at the margins can improve extraction, but it doesn't inevitably follow that the total amount extracted will reach infinity or some arbitrarily high value—the limit is how much was originally there in the first place, and scaling laws don't say anything about that. They focus only on statistical patterns derived from what we've extracted so far [^B5 [link](https://chatgpt.com/share/69a637ac-2bd8-8013-9ec5-e7a175be3252#:~:text=What%20Scaling%20Laws%20Do%20Not%20Say)].

Or consider this thought experiment: if we only train LLMs with material created by average 5-year-olds, even if we can scale infinitely, will we ever see adult-level reasoning or output quality? Probably not, and the same logic can be extended to superhuman outputs falling out of human inputs.

Now let's take a look at what's actually being measured by the people testing AI systems. Take a close look at the METR graph again, and you'll notice the y-axis measures time for a 50% chance of succeeding. A human employee who successfully completes tasks 50% of the time would be fired real quick. But ok, that's an unfair critique, I agree. They have an 80% graph as well. My point is that the graph _implies something_ about AI's growing abilities, but _it's not clear how that something actually relates to the value of human activity_. The details matter a lot here, because they determine what AI's limits actually are, whether AI will be an enhancement tool or a replacement, and how AI tools need to be used and audited. Maybe a 99% graph would be more useful, but would it have the same shape? Collaboration and integration between many teams is a core part of most human work, but how well can AI do it? What about how AI can score in the top tier on standardized tests—wouldn't it be more fair to compare to the performance of a human with internet access? (Or, if you believe overfitting in the training data is a problem, a human with the answer key?) The graph says nothing about any of this.

AI is advancing, no doubt. But not all forms of advancement lead to becoming "better" than humans. An AI that can only produce 90%-of-the-time-reliable work while getting faster and faster to the point that it is almost instantaneous is still not going to be better than a human in a problem space where any adequate speed is acceptable but high reliability is critical.

I think AI's revolutionary, general-purpose power boils down to just a few fundamental components after stripping away all of the ornamentation and plugins: human-like natural-language interface and processing, speed, and clever mapping of a "token prediction" process to non-linguistic domains, like images and audio. Beyond just text processing, NLP vastly increases ease of use (no more learning 50 buttons and 100 drop-down menu items, or a whole programming language, before you can do anything useful) and customizability (vibe coding). Speed has obvious impacts on R&D and productivity. And any artifact that can be reduced to some sort of structured stream of data can be ingested, reproduced, and refined. These building blocks will unlock vast opportunities as we begin to learn how to use them. But we also need to avoid getting carried away attributing more to AI's ability than it deserves.

In his article, Mr. Shumer expresses amazement at AI that not only codes up an app, but that also "opens the app itself, clicks through the buttons, tests the features, uses the app the way a person would." I'm sure watching it work is mesmerizing, but so is watching Selenium, a browser automation library available since 2004, run through a Selenese script. Similar tools for testing mobile apps have also been around for a long time. Another example that comes to mind is when a colleague asked me to try out a new ChatGPT image recognition feature. Attach a surreal image, like a man pressing shirts on an ironing board while sitting on his car trunk on a busy city street, and then ask, "what's weird about this picture?" You'll probably get a sensible answer, but I can also give you a sensible answer without using ChatGPT (though not in natural language). I'd take some open-source image recognition library and get a list of all objects in the scene, then train a basic ML model on a bunch of image keyword sets from Getty Images, then run the list against the model to determine which are the odd ones out. #city #street #cars is likely a common grouping in Getty Images; #city #cars #ironing_board is likely not. ML techniques like this simplified example predate ChatGPT by decades. I am _not_ saying that modern AI is using these "primitive" techniques under the hood—I'm sure that today's models have built their own original, inscrutable procedures for handling such problems somewhere in their billions and billions of parameters. I am pointing out that we need to keep AI accomplishments in perspective, comparing to what was already possible before AI. Seeing AI's achievements in sharp detail, rather than as just broad, blinding superiority in every imaginable human skill, will help us integrate AI into society in a sane, prudent, and minimally disruptive way.
