- covered well by Amodei

# (II) How will the ideas in this essay help society handle AI anxiety?
- build confidence

# (III) The pessimistic scenario - a "dark AI" future: rampant threats to physical security, and widespread economic subjugation
- AI is very powerful, thoroughly controlled by a few at the top of society, and mostly inaccessible to average people
- security: risks from external enemies, domestic terrorists, and countries engaging in abuses against their own citizens
- economic: total devaluation of all human labor (cognitive or otherwise) with no wealth/value redistribution system in place
- a casual observer will note this is a very common dystopian trope found in modern science fiction, and dystopian fiction has a long history of being quite popular, for some reason

# (IV) The physical world imposes limits
- AI is digital, "disembodied," making it easy to overlook how its inputs and outputs are dependent on the physical world
- examples include GPU/electricity/land shortages limiting AI availability and setting a price floor (or requiring continuing infusions from investors); real-world testing dragging iteration speed down (e.g. robot design); physical materials needing to be moved around in order to enable bioterrorism

# (V) Discovery is not advancement: extrapolation to infinity is unlikely
- classic xkcd ("Extrapolating"): <https://xkcd.com/605/>
- chatbots have fascinated people since ELIZA, making us more susceptible to optimism and misunderstanding the technology
- NL is a big leap and excites people, but other features are not major changes from before - just faster, and a lot easier to customize/integrate for a specific use case. example: what's weird about this image, could have been handled pre-LLM with object identifier and ML model trained on getty images tags that are likely to appear together
- output coming from input: underspecification can result in "generic" output; underspecification while also not really knowing what you want can lead to being impressed by generic output. e.g. "build me an app", reuse of existing ideas and could have been done with like an app building framework.
- speed is a significant differentiator, which certainly opens new possibilities and risks, but isn't the same as "completely better than humans" - there are many dimensions to "better", e.g. a chess engine that always produces its next move in 1 ms but loses 50% of its matches is not as good as Deep Blue beating a grandmaster but thinking an average of 3 minutes per move.
- METR measurements (the y-axis on the famous graph) and other commonly cited milestones like AP tests, MCAT etc. have limitations (50% success as a human = fired employee; task times don't measure teamwork / coordination; comparison is to "a human with internet access" not "a human test taker"; humans wrote the test so "AI vs a human" isn't the same as "AI vs humanity", where humans by definition always know 100% of the test since they also wrote the answer key, and so on...)
- figuring out "more hardware and more parameters means more model capability" could be similar to "more pumpjacks and more wells means more oil" - "all" the connections, knowledge, information, etc. is already there in the training corpus, generated by humans and filled with human limitations, and the training process "extracts" it, in much the same way as all the oil is already there in an oil field and the extraction hardware pulls it out. It is true that adding more hardware at the margins can extract more, but it doesn't inevitably follow that the total value extracted must be able to reach infinity or some arbitrarily high value.
- autonomous discovery can help push the boundaries of knowledge, but outside of specific domains like pure theoretical math, things that look good in theory need to be tested in practice - in the real world, as discussed in section (IV) - the knowledge is not necessarily valuable or even correct until then. Thought experiment: would you take an autonomously AI-developed drug (pill, liquid, etc.) before any testing or trials have been performed? Maybe AI will be good enough in 2 years, maybe 200, but my gut says it'll be on the longer end. And longer means more time, which means more opportunity to manage the post-scarcity transition properly. Or maybe AI will hit a real wall and never be good enough, which sadly means no post-scarcity, but it does mean our modern economic system won't fall completely apart and leave lots of people struggling to tread water.

# (VI) The pitfall of technological determinism: AI is not an alien invasion with its own thoughts and goals; its development and application are fully controlled by humans in society

# (VII) Entry-level people don't have to fill entry-level jobs, because the meaning of "entry-level job" is going to change
- just like with any job: it's a bundle of tasks, most likely some being automatable and some not
- job boundaries are flexible (as are job titles themselves) and tasks can be reorganized in a way that makes sense
- hiring young, ambitious, and AI-native people is path towards accumulating human capital and success for firms

# (VIII) Moonshots can focus higher-value labor and experienced workers
- keep your eye on the prize, think big, be highly ambitious: solve complex "moonshot" problems (lunar or Mars bases; space habitats; a cure for cancer; climate change; world hunger; nuclear fusion)
- Is giving a single initial prompt to an autonomous AI agent which has access to physical materials and the ability to orchestrate other agents good enough to solve the problem? If not, why not, and humans have to fill that gap, but how?
- Goals don't have to be so sweeping and grand; an individual worker can focus on their own firm and determine an ambitious goal relevant to the firm's core competencies.
- AI will learn human tasks making skills obsolete much more quickly than before. But this same "moonshot thought experiment" strategy can be applied again, using the new AI capabilities as a premise, to determine what the next set of human tasks will be.

# (IX) Handling the post-scarcity transition problem
- if AI hits a wall early, there will still be room for our economy to run much as it has been before, end of story
- if not, then a significantly large group of people (maybe everybody) will be permanently displaced from the labor market and we really do need a new economic paradigm
- an average career is 40 years; AI progress could be slow enough to give some a good career even as the process unfolds; slower also means more time to test and tune policy
- ideas like UBI or capital ownership are great for the end state, but the transition will be the real pain
- potential phenomena: deflation, falling asset prices, and falling GDP (due to labor devaluation, but "actual wealth" is going up, making these metrics misleading and in need of an overhaul - examples like parental childcare; water freely available from Earth instead of needing extraction; doing DIY home improvement instead of hiring labor); uneven casualties meaning some need early support - how to detect this (AISI "future of work" metric monitoring?); circular flow stagnation (Citrini: cost-cutting will not make all firms more profitable because they will also lose revenue with a big enough white-collar employment hit! This is a key observation which a lot of techno-optimists seem to be totally missing.)
- deflation is a key one, indicates something "bad" in normal circumstances but probably not bad in a post-scarcity transition - as Altman points out the lower price level makes everyone "richer".

# (X) Optimism is justified
- likely scenario #1: AI is similar to prior revolutions, advances in physical domains brings about whole new jobs and job categories we can't imagine today, and our future economic system looks similar to today's
- likely scenario #2: AI really will push us into a post-scarcity future, where AI is superior to humans at essentially all forms of valuable labor - _but at a slow enough pace to give ourselves time to adapt policy to support the transition as well as the end state_. Major reasons as stated above: limits to LLM capabilities will show up somewhere, eventually; flexibility in jobs and job responsibilities; ambitious, physical-world projects that fit into the existing structure of corporations, profit, labor, and that AI will struggle to accomplish 100% autonomously.
- in either case, the transition will be painful for some (or everyone); proper policy design is critical for getting people through the transitional state without too much unrest or civilization collapsing
- in case of scenario #2, proper policy design for the post-scarcity end state is also needed
