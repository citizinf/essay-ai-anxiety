# main points
- 🌑 start with "dark AI" assumptions: centralized and fully powerful tech that makes human cognition obsolete (inferior for most use cases) - demand loss, feudalism, apocalyptic concerns I'm sure you've seen plenty of recently.
  - Assume we manage to create the perfect humanoids. Replacements for actual humans physically and cognitively but less needs and don't need payment. No robot uprising risks. Then this is the _pessimistic_ economic scenario because human labor has lost value in such a world and human labor has been for centuries (market economy) been used to distribute resources (economic/scarcity) in a manner deemed "fair". Results in instability, etc. (see 3 outcomes Autor). The analysis is about seeing if AI really is a manifestation of such humanoids, or not.
  - what really makes autocracies dangerous, but democracies are not immune (other issues are softer/commercial power, e.g. <https://www.cnbc.com/2026/02/16/chinas-tech-shock-ai-monopoly-us.html> but not something I've thought carefully about)
  - tweak assumptions, see what happens: what if model mini makes it possible for everyone to have on phone, overuse with no limits? imperfect cognition replacement so collab is better? Basically: not as dark, e.g. path of the transistor. Also, I argue this path is more likely already, AND we can as a society direct our future along this path.
- 🔨 physical world limits
  - GPU shortage
  - monitoring/control of data flows in/out of data centers (assuming continued centralization of inference)
  - iteration time: robots, growing food, 9 women and 1 baby
  - delivering a pile of uranium / biolab: we have framework and systems for detection
- 🌐tech determinism: how we plan integration into existing systems
  - food ordering / nuke launching automation example: unpredictability means we obviously just shouldn't hook AI up this way. simpler examples would be giving full control of your personal devices, depending on AI output to be accurate in important cases e.g. "make sure we covered every item on the safety checklist" when reviewing a mountain climber's plan, for example, using AI email inbox sort and missing important emails.
  - EXAMPLE of danger: openclaw; with full access, imagine a virus that packages openclaw in it and runs commands like delete user files or open a sensitive webpage (you already are logged in of course) to obtain info, wreak havoc on govt or corporate systems, etc. Any openclaw like system should immediately be disabled by OS level antivirus by default.
  - But the incentives will be too high, pressure is going to be unavoidable I think. It's either take the risk or die at the hands of competitors who are taking the risks, squeaking past 99.99% of the time, and getting more business/results at a lower cost than you. So: external-to-the-model safeguards must be designed well and required. (Not just post-training which still retains a black-box, unpredictable feeling.) Cost of failure matters a lot. In cases where failure doesn't really matter (e.g. if an email is really important someone will try other ways of contacting you) maybe the sub-100 performance risk is fine. In cases where it really does, I think we should treat AI automation differently than drop-in human interaction: AI's "workspace" should be designed so that a coherent but insane person put in that workspace cannot do unacceptable damage. (And also an incoherently insane person.)
  - many examples of how "listening to the computer" can lead to issues: facial recognition by police depts, mortgage/insurance claim approval, etc.
  - e.g. incentives in the opposite direction, like airline chatbot claim of refunds that don't actually exist; Canadian court says the company must honor
- 🎓 entry level is not entry level (entry-level people don't need to perform entry-level work)
  - just because some tasks are learned by AI (drawing hands) does not mean "everything, all at once, and soon" will be handled by AI - all 3 of those can be questioned
  - more generally: boundaries of job responsibilities are not immobile - highly specialized "general cognitive" professions are an organizational choice; we have many cultural and policy levers to play with
  - autor: Atlantic collab vs automate; paper expertise
  - in school: learn the basics to understand AI outputs, then don't waste time at work doing work AI does faster. E.g. arithmetic & calculators. What's different: black box, unreliability/unpredictability, a "veneer" of thoroughness and confidence
  - Also give firms advice for using AI: those who learn how to harness entry level ambition, ai native, fresh perspective, and willingness to work hard for relatively low pay will find success in the future. Fluid vs crystallized intelligence. Who's better at learning new buttons on the next smartphone OS and who's resistant to learning? Building expertise in a world that has powerful tools available will eventually be more valuable expertise than existing expertise built without those tools. And office politics, which can entrench those with power already - correlated with expertise, but not the same - is not really a productive form of expertise at least at the societal level. Firms are not gods pushing workers around like ants, we don't only need to give workers advice on protecting themselves from cost-cutting firms.
  - need to work past defensiveness of the entrenched higher level workers: "I worked hard for my position, you need to work hard for yours" even if what AI means is that the work that "newbies" usually do just doesn't make sense to waste much time on anymore. related: rules like "you need 2 years experience to be considered for promo"
- 📈 discovery is not enhancement: extrapolation may be wrong
  - NL interface is a big leap and captures imagination
  - lots of other stuff (detect what's weird about this picture) already doable by computers
  - AI profile (fast, high volume) is also already a computer profile
  - What do "AI scaling laws" actually mean? <https://chatgpt.com/c/69928626-f19c-8328-87cf-34ecd99ed0f9>
  - What does "smarter than a human" actually mean? There are many dimensions. The ones where AI is "smarter" may not matter much, like a calculator is faster at specific tasks but so what. Or are we comparing "all AIs as a whole" vs "a single human"? It's not clear that something trained only on human level data can, say, start posing new unsolved questions and then solving them.
- 🚀 moonshots / "keep your eye on the prize": highlight AI gaps
  - technical skills still matter, significantly. We can't just dump all hope onto soft skills which are hard to judge and measure, giving benefit to those good at office politics, which is not societally productive.
  - examples of things that might be considered fundamental AI shortcomings (not like Amodei fingers example): the need for initial conditions setup, context, and prompting
  - research "case studies": companies advertising, bragging about successes: do this with ai agents. Or, academic case studies on business successes/failures.
  - Many different dimensions to moonshots: speed; anticipating human behavior if relevant (e.g. give me the most effective marketing campaign in history, write a "good" novel, ...); just technical (build a rocket that can make it to Mars and keep a dog alive); picking success metrics (what does "eradicate world poverty" mean?); etc. - these are different kinds of achievements and target the problem of "cognitive labor devaluation" differently. E.g. struggling through an innovative technical achievement in the physical world may unlock new industries, jobs, etc; increasing the speed/efficiency of an existing process probably exacerbates the issue of higher productivity with no change in employment / cognitive labor demand.
  - ⭐example workflow: (1) a problem or something with room for improvement is identified; (2) existing or new firms, researchers, etc. take a first pass at fixing it, using AI brainstorming and prototyping; (3) a whole bunch of random crap just doesn't work; (4) humans fix up the things that don't work; (5) the idea is productionized, maybe with AI help; (6) profit.
  - if you look closely, you'll see this workflow is barely different from what capitalism has already enabled for us in the past 200 years, but I think AI can greatly increase the pace of it.
  - separately: AI can be used to quickly learn the human work in (4), so continued innovation will be necessary for existing firms to stay in business and for existing employees to keep their jobs.
  - also: AI brainstorming can eventually extend into AI-enabled physical world prototyping, if we get enough advances in robotics. I don't think that's anywhere close to prime time right now, but hey look! There's a business opportunity for you, AI robotics, enhanced to actually be useful in the workflow above.
  - the skeptic would say (3) won't happen and AI can do everything. I think that's wildly optimistic and maximalist, and also depends heavily on the scope of (1). (history of optimism: rodney brooks on "no more code" in 1963; self driving cars; electric cars like golf carts as my shop teacher said, etc.)

- ai: on entry level, thought experiment
  - entry level is not a charity. firms hire to get actual work done. analogy to factories: machine operators are least powerful, lowest paid, easy to cut in times of financial stress, but also the factory completely doesn't work without them, which cannot be said as straightforwardly of the managers. try to discount "value added to firm" double counting. if you're a white collar manager with 10 "entry level" directs, each generates 100k in value (new sales leads etc) for the company, let's count that as 100k per worker and NOT 1M for the manager. now if a firm were actually to try to get rid of those workers to save on salary and boost margins, what would happen? EVERY SINGLE gap between the 2 cases is, at least with current model capabilities, something that requires human expertise and that you can hone in on.
  - the smartest firms who see real growth opportunities, not just ones who want to boost margins and buy back stock so the new CEO can finally become a billionaire like his peers (founder-replacement CEOs) will reframe this as an opportunity to "make each entry level leaf node a manager" by harnessing what AI is good at while providing the human expertise. yes that category will change rapidly as models progress, but young people are a lot better at adapting to changing technology. think about smartphone updates... is the 50 year old good at figuring out where the new buttons are every year? now boost that to every month, who is going to be able to keep on top of this stuff?
  - white collar "lowest rung" is still a lot better than general blue collar, don't get a victim mentality and expect the same empathy as working poor. you're rich and smart enough to use the analogy to give yourself an advantage in the workplace.

- ai: the counterintuitive, cognitively dissonant strategy to follow for job security
  - simultaneously try to push AI to its limits in taking over your job, while also honing in on the parts that still require human expertise and developing those skills. need to **be honest with yourself** (the hardest part psychologically) or you will fail to prepare optimally (or at all). (and think about if the AI shortcomings are fundamental to the tech, or not. e.g. fingers example from Amodei, vs. the need for initial setup, context, and prompting preventing full autonomous) -- moonshots/think big; don't focus on just your job but the overarching picture of "what value does my firm/org create for society" and how can it be done better / faster / cheaper?

- ai: reviewable examples - AI trades off high speed for worse accuracy
  - drawing: high effort, easily reviewed (does it look good); text generation less so (review by human may cause human to miss something that would have been written if done manually)
  - radiology ?
  - summarizing long documents: it depends: if your "review" entails reading the entire summary AND reading the entire original doc to make sure every single part of the summary is accurate, that's not much better - and if your goal was just to get a summary (so the nonai cost would be skimming the doc) and not write the summary (so the nonai cost would already have reading the whole original doc baked in) then it's actually worse. Having the right scaffolding, i.e. to connect all parts of the summary with actual original doc sources, is valuable.
  - other examples... software

- This is kind of a management problem at core: how to confirm "employees" are actually doing work; hidden costs / "tech debt" that come from supervising instead of seeing every detail; how the dynamics might change with AI; mindful control to make sure AI speed is not depended on overconfidently while increasing certain costs or introducing new ones

- ai key reviewability point: level of expertise required to GENERATE something no longer tied to expertise required to AUDIT something. How can inexperienced workers develop auditing skills if they are pressured to make generation the priority?
