# Goals
- Having a deep and nuanced understanding of AI's societal impacts can help us (humanity as a whole) feel more in control as the technology develops - helping people feel comfortable with the idea we should "steer the bus" (as Mr. Amodei says) and confident that we will be able to do so effectively.
- Emphasis / concurrence with several points raised by Amodei in "defenses" sections, and why we should view some of them with high confidence as practical, doable, and not too disruptive. (My intention is not to nurture complacency, it's to stave off defeatism and give society the psychological push it needs to get started on these solutions.)

# Idea tidbits
- Some fear around _labor market disruption_ i.e. people won't be able to find jobs. Remember Keynes from 100 years ago, predict people will work only 15 hours a week. Keynes would be doubly surprised, (1) that people still work a lot more than 15h, but (2) that once a technology comes along that plausibly, due to its general & widespread reach as "general-purpose cognitive replacement for humans," seems make it possible to provide basic needs for the modern world so efficiently that most people don't need to / can't find work, that people would fear it instead of celebrating. Of course, we all understand why ("how will I pay rent") but this is an economic/policy question about how our economy is designed & managed.
  - actually, Keynes's views were significantly more nuanced, and the prospect of only having to work 15 hours / week "filled him with dread", though not for reasons that match today's economic anxieties around AI. <https://www.aei.org/op-eds/is-affluence-a-barrier-to-living-well> (seems to be more concerned with a "spiritual" problem of abundance replacing the "struggle for subsistence" rather than an inability for significant majority to access the abundance).
  - anyway, Keynes was wrong about population growth, the growth rate from 1925-2025 was even higher than 1825-1925. Maybe that kept the "struggle for subsistence" alive for a bit longer, making Keynes ahead of his time. (He still missed the distributional problem though.)
- _lump of labor / size of the pie fallacies_: building off the 99% widgets machine example, theoretically laid-off people can start other economic endeavors, i.e. their own companies. Massively increased economic size does not necessarily equal 0 human involvement; this may just be a rebalancing of how many employees per profit unit the average company has.
- Does AI never regress or _forget_? This a human weakness and if present in AI can drag it back from superhuman status. There are 2 aspects to this: "forgetting" in the sense of something falling out of the context window, and "forgetting" in the sense that newly trained model versions regress on some skills that older versions were better at (possibly things that aren't measured well by public benchmarks, which clearly suffer from overfitting risk).
- AI performs well, but does it perform well enough to _prompt itself_? Using AI agentic coding, in my experience prompt engineering is more subtle than a skeptic might first assume. LLM-based AI seems to have a critical core built around the need for a good input prompt, but can't humans be in the loop there? Many examples of bad prompts highlight this problem well ([source?]): the vast majority of prompts are very **underspecified**; and the model's decisions to fully specify it can pull the output towards feeling bland, generic, like "slop." (I'd assume this is by design, for example, the request "tell me a bedtime story" can technically map on to either of "tell me a good bedtime story" or "tell me a boring, crappy bedtime story", but any model that has undergone basic post-training and alignment is almost sure to pick the first option over the second.) For me, when I want to use AI coding to help implement something that faithfully matches a design in my head (whether that's front-end work or architectural design), the size of all my prompts themselves + customized general instructions like AGENTS.md or skills, in bytes, is sometimes larger than the code output in bytes. (Not counting the huge wall of built-in instructions; reasoning tokens; non-code explanatory output; correcting mistaken prompt inputs; etc.) That's definitely a noisy metric dependent on the specific task I was working on and my own prompting skill, but the fact remains that **significant human effort is put into crafting inputs**. Similarly, we talk about how models are getting better and better at performing well on benchmarks or tests, but who is writing those tests and who is writing the answer keys? Should AI advance to the point of self-training and self-benchmarking, will the numbers still be trustworthy? What I'm saying is that, maybe, fundamentally (despite AI breaking our assumptions about its "fundamental" limitations at a blistering pace), the fact that humans are significantly involved with the inputs, both during training and usage, makes a big difference to how "good" AI actually is, and something unfixable will break once AI is given control over all the inputs and outputs.
- Belief that AI may disadvantage the _"lower intellectual ability"_ humans first could be backwards. If AI is "smarter than a Nobel laureate in every field" and a "general purpose cognitive replacement for humans", aren't people who rely on intelligence to make a living in the economy the most disadvantaged? Is there an implicit assumption here that AI will reach a ceiling above which it's possible for the "smartest" humans to be and thus continue useful knowledge work?
- _technological determinism_: It's not AI itself that will do things, it's how humans use it. There is a component of human nature (drop a knife in a crowd in an anarchy and see what happens), but also deliberate design (many examples: nukes, smartphones/social media/internet on teenagers, facial recognition software with confidence intervals that cause counterintuitive statistical interpretation problems like Simpson's paradox, conflating sensitivity/specificity, and just in general the idea that "99% confident" is not the same as "proven guilty"). Cybersecurity experts know the internet is a place hiding many weaknesses, like a city neighborhood with lots of unlocked windows and side doors. So we shouldn't just let autonomous, self-prompting AI do anything it wants on the internet. Fine - but note this is a **human decision** about **how to apply a technology**, not something that an alien has forced upon us.
- (related to _technological determinism_ and _self-prompting_) AI _needs to be "used"_; unlike the risks of say a viral biological agent, which spreads without human control, AI itself in its current format has to have humans actively choose to use it. Of course bad actors can try to use AI to create such threats, like a computer or biological virus, but I'm not convinced that's the same class of problem as "runaway AI". Perhaps one guardrail can be to map out the "attack surface" revealed by any new AI capability, and categorize that capability into offensive and defensive parts. We could say, for example, that responsible AI development requires explicit disclosure of this full analysis and also clear proof that defensive capabilities meet or exceed offensive capabilities; and for example limit the ability of untrusted or "private" actors to access GPU compute warehouses (or GPUs themselves) if they don't share this data. E.g. an AI that is good enough to make a disabling widespread computer virus should also be good enough to guard assets from a disabling widespread computer virus. a hand wavy argument along the lines can also be made for biology and bioweapons. we're behind right now, e.g. COVID response time and many other existing and potential infectious diseases we can't control, but it may be the case in the future that AI which understands human biology well enough to make bioweapons can also understand human biology well enough to guard against bioweapons. The issue may be destructive potential of the technology. Asymmetry: we know how to build and use nukes, but if one is falling above your head right now it's not clear we have any serious way to stop it from killing you. There may also be ways for AI to change this dynamic though. I have a pet theory that there may be something of an inverse correlation between the "marginal capital intensity" of producing a particular weapon (i.e. how many physical resources are required to specifically produce that instance of that weapon) vs how easy it is to defend (cyber is low marginal capital intense since all the GPUs are already there for other reasons and the real inputs are like raw electricity and time, and due to its abstract nonphysical nature it feels easier to defend against than falling nukes). This can help to balance out real-world risks.
- AI compute is _not limitless_. It's not like how we're all used to Google being free and fast and always available 24/7. There are limits, data centers are getting built to handle load, etc. Living too much in the digital world as many of us do, perhaps especially those at the frontier of AI development, can make us discount the _physical limits of reality_. (e.g. alien movies nautilus article: where is all the energy/food coming from to make aliens so fast & violent?) This necessarily puts limits (though perhaps hard for us to predict/calibrate them) as to what a "country of 50 million superhumans" can actually do.
- AI being digital & just knowledge: also a limit for bad actors, e.g. terrorists who want to create a biological or nuclear weapon without governmental approval. This is _not just a knowledge problem_, it is physical too. I can't prompt ChatGPT to conjure up a pile of uranium (or fertilizer) for me in a manner that is fully digital and undetectable. Of course for cyber e.g. disinformation there's more leeway, but we already see that (so AI does not necessarily make the threat "worse" or mutate it into something different and more sinister in kind). Similar reasoning can be applied to particular domains like biotech, nanotech, manufacturing, nuclear fusion, robotics (see first half chatgpt chat <https://chatgpt.com/g/g-p-693c5fbcfddc8191b85e533796262c32-ai-on-ai/c/69720680-d28c-8333-b543-1ad886087a65>)
- another human nature concern: people will still trust the review of human experts in _critical domains where human lives are at risk_. NASA's slow and extremely expensive "product cycle" (and its exposure to the physical realm, and the massive cost of a single failure, ...) is unlikely to be **fully** (i.e. 100%, not 99% like the widgets example) AI-automated, and definitely not on the same timeline as other software or firmware engineering tasks. Also consider medical software (what a pacemaker runs), self-driving cars, architectural planning, etc. It's not necessarily the case that "products that included humans in the loop at some point during development are better" (AI might really be better), but that "people value and place greater trust in products that had human oversight." This is not just some sort of vague subconscious phenomenon either. For example, legal responsibility may flow through humans in a way that just doesn't apply to AI models. Consider how the MIT License (commonly used and regarding legal rights similar to most software licenses in use today) includes an "as-is" clause that basically says, "if you use this code and something bad happens, that's on you, not on the people who wrote this code". As digital products, LLMs will at least somewhat be subject to the same protections. And even if that is earnestly overturned by regulation, say, what will the alternative be **in a world of fully autonomous development**? Does every case go through the court system (relevant: lawsuits against OpenAI due to teen suicide)? Will AI companies establish a "victim fund" much like trucking and transportation companies, which "fixes" the legal problem without fixing the actual human life cost (or root cause AI problem)? Is the AI company at fault or is it the company that used AI in building an unsafe product (remember, in this world both of these companies may have "board of directors"-style owners but no employees)?
- Saying that _entry-level work_ is at risk can _hide the distinction between entry-level people and entry-level work_. It's easier to illustrate if I simplify a bit: suppose everyone who has less than 2 years of working experience total (i.e. new grads) is considered an "entry-level employee." Let's suppose AI is powerful enough to consistently perform 90% of what's considered 2025 entry-level work. ... 2 definitions of entry level: any work given to new employee vs "AI style work". not necessarily identical anymore. new employees will be "AI native", have used AI since young, have a better sense of what's it's good at and how to use it (when did it help with my homework and when not), and a better sense of shit testing too. In a lower stakes environment (school). draw parallels to computer, internet, social media (think about how disinformation stereotypically affects older people more, not used to seeing "published" content) (the idea that computers can spout confident shit may be new to old people, but it's a common human behavior. schoolkids will see people and AI spout shit and learn quickly about hallucination detection and dangers, just like not listening to the bully who tells you eating soap will turn you into a superhero) - for entry level, saying all the work is "too hard" is a lack of imagination, consider how apprenticeships work in domains like arch and construction

- drawing a _parallel with mechanization_: some labor erased by machines & elec & robotics, but much still isn't. e.g. high skill manufacturing, or low skill e.g. hotel maid cleaning (human dexterity makes it way not cost-effective to replace maids with robots). Are cognitive jobs more like A (enhanced with ai), B (fundamentally apart from ai but also "easy" to fill so low wages), or something else like totally replaceable? it's not immediately obvious that "a lot is totally replaceable"
- how replaceable work is with AI also depends on how _reviewable_ the work is. This is connected to dependability / error rate. If a trained human can be expected to make almost no mistakes, but an AI can make subtle mistakes, that helps with job security. But if it's easy to look over the work and catch mistakes, that's not good for job security - like Doctorow's idea with the radiologist except it's easier to catch stuff. If reviewing the work takes as long, or longer, than performing the work itself, that indicates room for improvement in how the work is done but also may be an intrinsic property of the work [ask ChatGPT for examples]. E.g. software is "easy to test" because you just click a button and see if it works (more complex failure modes are possible but this is already standard practice in the industry, not new AI issue, this is one of the reasons why engineers are kept around, someone needs to fix breaking issues that come up in production. So basically software is more exposed because of its ease of reviewing work + "new" kinds of AI failures are similar to in-prod failures we're already familiar with and understand)
- biorisk: issue is with "hiding relevant information" - consider how encryption/security works with computers - no part of the technique is hidden, yet it still works due to a particular kind of math problem being hard.
- measuring agentic AI capabilities by task length like METR is somewhat misguided. this doesn't take into account parallelism. if it takes a single human 100 years to code the next version of iOS from scratch, that doesn't say much about how well a big team at Apple can do it, and how fast. The question of how well a team of many independent AI agents can work together and then _integrate their work_ is not answered, and integration is a huge pain point in software. Just ask forward deployed engineers.
- AI shortcomings / jagged frontier: Anthropic's project vend; assumptions about what's easy or hard (value of labor) are built on top of assumptions of what humans find easy or hard; AI doesn't have exactly the same profile, and those differences and how they play out in employment & wages is not well understood yet. e.g. hotel maids still a thing due to simplicity of human dexterity vs complexity of robotics. some human cognitive features may become important defenses for us as well, e.g. "generally not going randomly insane after some amount of work" (see nytimes bing ai convo; Gemini please die; project vend)
- ⭐My optimistic scenario: vigorous competition leading to significant margin compression for the winning firms (there must be some winners, without a robot uprising the process would not have a reason to proceed without some winners, at least outside governmental interests like defense which suffers from game theoretic arms race issues where everyone is worse off) (dependent on AI actually being easily democratized, miniaturized/distilled like rapid development across different firms, open weight models, SLMs, etc. suggest). winners could be identified by, e.g. revenue per employee, sure there will be details to work out, and businesses like ads from Google/Meta may be unfairly targeted, though generally the companies that are "unfairly" targeted here are likely in the best position to take advantage of AI rather than being disrupted by it. margin compression lowers price levels in a widespread way which is a market-driven way to make everyone better off (things like UBI are subject to political instability, many insoluble/subjective questions of fairness). this also tilts the progression more in the direction of innovation, as opposed to cost-cutting, as Amodei mentioned, both will be needs, but policy/incentives can shape which one gets the focus. Basically build in a preference for R&D over operations when it comes to human involvement in the running of a firm. competition also provides more opportunities for humans to sell their labor. also: physical world advancements which can actually create new unimagined classes of jobs, rather than just replacing humans (supported by governmental subsidies maybe). Basic components:
  - incentivize innovation over cost-cutting (innovation in physical domains can actually lead to new jobs, not just a pure cognitive substitute - it's just coincidental that past "revolutions" also created new kinds of jobs)
  - encourage vigorous competition to maintain labor demand and compress winning firm margins ("your margin is my opportunity") ("vigorous competition" idea is not new at all, that's what we have antitrust for; there's a long academic and practical history of trying to balance rewarding business owners & risk takers & value creators, vs ensuring corporations operate in such a way to serve the public good. Metrics exist too, like tracking labor share of national income since 1947)
  - it's not a prisoners dilemma hopefully; some orgs will be winners. target them not for punitive taxes necessarily but for directing profits towards societal welfare at large (e.g. innovative and margin-compressing reinvestment), not enriching those who are already rich, so they can double the sizes of their houses, double the number of houses, buy bigger yachts, etc.

# Philosophical musings
- Leisure and decision making: humans are likely to continue to _want to make decisions_ in some cases. Like would I allow AI to order all my food and do all my grocery shopping? Or decide for me what TV I should watch? Imagine if Netflix revamped their UI to remove all the content browsing screens, and the only thing that's left is autoplay. (And no you don't get a skip button. AI knows you. AI knows when you want to skip. And you don't want to skip. So be a good human and watch what I'm showing you.) Outside of extremely abnormal conditions, we'll never get 100% of humanity to like this approach. Tiktok is close but still offers plenty of room for human agency. The point is that there are fundamental gaps to the story "AI does everything" and considering these gaps deeply and seriously can help us to understand how far-reaching AI should become, and what our goals for controlling the process should be.
- autonomy: consider this contrived example: a country's nuclear weapons command center uses computers to control whether to launch nukes. Employees also have internet access in order to order lunch delivery. A new app is installed on the on-premises servers that has a 99% chance of ordering food for employees in a way that's more convenient than how a 2025 human is used to on an app (e.g. maybe it counts the number of people who need food, tracks dietary restrictions/preferences, keeps tabs on surge pricing, etc.). It has a 1% chance of instead accessing the nuke control computers and launching nukes. Should this app be allowed to continue operating? The issue here is having the right environmental setup for this app to cause serious problems in the first place. Even without the app, for example, people might be rightly frightened by the fact it's even possible to have a single node be able to access BOTH the internet (which also includes receiving data from the internet) as well as the nuke launch computers. This actually shouldn't be possible.
- seems like cybersecurity will be wrapped even more tightly with AI than other firms, even software firms - will be tied to basic R&D relating to autonomous, agentic web behavior. _maybe_ more safe from AI than other jobs. (look at cyber safety ratings from LLM labs)
- is there a foundational assumption about people "needing" to work 40 hours in order to earn their wealth and be able to support the basics, with higher-value work also unlocking luxuries like a bigger house, vacations, etc.? I don't think it's that simple - I saved lots of money working in big tech and have a sizable passive income stream, and have been supporting a family on the passive income alone (+ wife's passive income). This isn't unusual at all, people retire and do it all the time. I am younger than retirement age but this fits in with the recent FIRE movement. Perhaps in the future, "everyone" can be in the landlord/rentier class, and this can be tuned to be enough for the basics, and people can choose to work or make risky investments to attempt to get more money for luxuries? It's just UBI by another name, and one that operates through capital markets rather than governments. See Autor/Thompson essay on UBC and WI (raindrop #ai): Beyond Job Displacement: How AI Could Reshape the Value of Human Expertise
- human contribution to major tasks (moonshots): considering the size of the task, in addition to other stuff mentioned in Amodei essay and my response: if my prompt is "design a universal cancer cure drug, use a factory to produce 1000000 pills, and have a delivery drone place one on my desk after completion", is this feasible? maybe in 500 years, 100, even 50, but a whole human career is only 40 and that leaves plenty of time for adjustment. consider other moonshots, THINK BIG, people will still be needed: targeted immunotherapy per person (capability AND speed), solving world hunger, general global/3rd-world economic development, lunar and Mars base expansion, ... is any of this possible with a single prompt to a "driver agent" basically CEO AI which controls other agents? --- There are 2 aspects to this. 1 are people needed to operate such technology if it exists or is fully autonomous driver agent AI actually possible? 2 are people needed to get AI to this point? assuming AI does not reach full capability in all domains at exactly the same time, doesn't this leave room for people work?

Related: what if we create new jobs but AI agents can outcompete people for them (general cognitive substitute)?
- _AI Revolution vs Industrial Revolution_: there's a key difference and we can't get complacent assuming widespread benefits to society in the same way as IR: modern economy is much more exposed to potential _demand loss_ after disruption. <https://chatgpt.com/g/g-p-693c5fbcfddc8191b85e533796262c32-ai-on-ai/c/6988fe5a-6b60-832b-919e-86c31474960d>
  - related chain of thought: can this harm the economy: it's not just about productivity per capita, but the reshaping of flows. even if GDP increases, consider circular flow model: if most value transfer is between AI companies and agents (agents don't need money to operate, but electricity etc.), then most people will see less flows which feels like contracting economy even if overall is bigger. blunt statistics like GDP will be completely the wrong metrics to focus on in such a scenario. _I encourage economists to consider what metrics to monitor / new metrics to design in order to track AI disruption as it happens, so we're not left guessing and listening to theoretical discussions from random internet commenters._ (see metrics section)
- I think one of the biggest changes with future cognitive labor, in case there still is room for humans to do something, is that skills will just not be static. We already basically see a version of this e.g. if you want a 40-year career as a software developer, even before genAI/AGI, you clearly need a different skillset at different points: passing classes in university, the first decade, the next decade, etc. Powerful-enough AGI will quickly make cognitive skills "obsolete" at least in terms of humans being able to outcompete AI; BUT if development moves at a breakneck speed then maybe humans will still be needed for a variety of things like NYTimes article - whether it's AI steering, precise mechanical repairs, fixing foundational/infra issues, management of the social layer (stuff outside the AI economy, e.g. politicans). Basically AGI will always be coming to displace humans, but if we continue to target the "moonshots" and distill out the tasks that still require humans AND that humans are willing to do (i.e. not menial/dangerous e.g. go into space or underwater and fix this or that) we may still be able to provide "some" work for some class of people.
- _"dark AI"_ (lightly discussed in a ChatGPT chat) where a large chunk of "not smart enough" society cannot outcompete AI for any cognitive jobs; leaving only the "bad" non-automatable jobs, government redistribution, or passive income from capital: I worry this could become stable and entrenched, much like how feudalism lasted half a millennium. People could arguably be worse off than today on average even with continuing developments, but _maybe not_ depending on what goals AI can achieve (automated homebuilding? science/health/medicine improvements? good entertainment but not addicting?) - though this still heavily falls into an "elite control" situation.
- _digital media/entertainment is saturated_: Some ideas around building a leisure-focused economy or moving workers out of "productive" industries into leisure industries will be IMO a small adjustment with little effect. Especially in the digital realm, media/entertainment is very saturated: This isn't like 100 years ago where most of life is drudgery or having "ad hoc" fun with your local community, maybe reading the newspaper, and attending a play or choir concert every month or so. If you just look at all the TV/videos being produced today, we've reached a point where there's enough to last a lifetime produced every year, or something. Analogy: past development is like moving from loincloths to a full wardrobe: true value produced starting from a point of scarcity. Reapplying that % growth to future development is like moving from a wardrobe to a warehouse: the value provided & therefore the amount a nonrich person is willing to pay is no longer there; a single person can't use a warehouse of clothes; very obviously economic _diminishing returns_. The current digital media ecosystem is already far along the spectrum towards "warehouse"; value comes from curation now, which is more artistic and therefore exclusionary, with the most popular people getting almost all the value. ("the etsy economy" will not work in practice)
- _powerful AI_ maximally optimal projections miss parallels with prior: when a "new" thing is discovered we may pin more hopes on it than what actually pans out. parallel with original discovery of crude oil: claims about e.g. its medicinal benefits, can be used to cure diseases etc. sure now there's Vaseline but today these claims seem absurd to us. of course oil has become one of the most critically important discoveries/inputs/technologies of our time, but it doesn't have benign, let alone positive, health effects. (also: overestimation of capabilities, self driving cars / full autopilot - see YouTube vid)
- keeping in mind historical failures like Cliff Stoll's prediction the internet will fail; Krugman on internet and fax machines: Is gen/agentic AI really going to open up _whole new categories of work_? Unlike e.g. oil, elec, it does not modify the physical world. Computers were more about changing the speed of information creation, transfer, processing, making things like animated movies, livestreaming, etc. possible. (Also: networking and collation - e.g. compared to TV broadcasting, livestreaming out looks the same, but audience interaction coming in is computer-speed-enabled; centralizing all viewers into 1 chat roll would be unworkable in the past.) If AI offers neither physical change or increased speed (beyond what the jump from analog->computers already did), perhaps it won't change much - we could instead hope for indirect effects like advances in (the 5 areas I discussed with ChatGPT), e.g. changes in nanotech or fusion really would reshape our world in a way SF currently dreams of.
- _Cost-cutting vs innovation_ incentive as Amodei discusses in his essay: if my position is really that innovation is the best long term strategy and what "smart" firms will do and succeed at, why are "so many" firms taking a cost-cutting approach instead? Is it noise from the fact of existing economic conditions e.g. uncertainty and high interest rates? I think taking a realist view is important. Firms make decisions that are best given their current interests and info. Many decision makers are owners (shareholders) and financialization means they will make decisions that test to boost the stock price. The stock market observably has short-termism built into it by overweighting the probability that any near-term change will wildly affect future cash flows/profitability, justifiable from an investor's point of view (goal is to make money) even if those probabilities are inaccurate (research idea: list all of the developments that have caused a 10% change in a stock's price, and analyze if those individual developments actually changed cash flows 10/20 years out by 10% or more from what they would have been otherwise). ANALOGY: homeowner NIMBYism and asset value sheltering by restricting development. There's widespread agreement that "what's best for society" means building more, and more affordable, housing. I mean real macroeconomic affordable housing, not just feel-good token projects adding 500 apartments a year in SF, which has the feel of the Super Bowl ad that cost $10m to brag about raising $800k for charity (or whatever those numbers were). But it just isn't happening because of entrenched interests, I think _interacting with the legal and regulatory environment_. The current incentivization of cost-cutting to increase stock prices (which, unlike what neoliberal boosters highlight, has neutral effects at best and negative effects elsewhere - this is not investment to generate value, it is an attempt to capture more of existing value by sharing less with those instrumental in creating it but without power to make decisions on its allocation) suggests that changes might needed in the regulatory environment. That might mean passing new laws to limit the appeal of such incentives; it may also mean updating or overturning old laws that juice/feed these incentives. Hopefully this framing can prevent the issue from becoming a US-partisan one and keep it focused on intellectual analysis. Regarding "class warfare", Amodei's point of avoiding an angry mob producing bad policy, plus democracy means 1-person-1-vote and if the rich stay as a a small minority, they structurally have a disadvantage when it comes to political power - as long as the non-rich don't all cancel out each other's votes over issues that with regard to importance, pale in comparison to AI risks we face.
- The "_transition problem_" is similar to inflation in a certain respect: the before-state and end-state might be stable and tolerable, but the in-between is not. Naively (as I did before experiencing 2022, and I'm young enough to have not experienced this before), one can think of inflation as just changing the numbers with no effect on life, like if we just replaced the USD with the "US Cent" as the main unit of currency and basically end up with the same economy but with numbers that look more like yen. (In real life inflation is not totally nominal of course, things like supply/demand play into costs rising. And different things' prices are affected differently. But purely numerical effects are significant too. Imagine the government decides to print tons of money and 100x the money supply, to, I dunno, pay off debts, or maybe stimulate something... it would be just a perfect USD->USC replacement. But do you want to be caught in the middle?) Related thought experiment from when I was younger, what if literally everything in the universe were suddenly doubled in size, every single atom... no difference right? But the pain is felt DURING inflation, not after it, when prices spike quickly and people start taking higher paying jobs elsewhere in July but your company's annual pay raise process only happens in April. Similarly, if a post-scarcity future really is in store for us, then of course some sort of well-designed UBI or UBC would need to be in place so people can access the goods/services they need/want, and saying we need to think about UBI design now is fine, but the _process of getting there_, unemployment while no good UBI yet exists, while goods/services are still priced at price levels that bake in expected costs due to labor, capital returns/margins, etc... can cause serious trouble. At "paycheck-to-paycheck" style living, like a lot of people apparently experience, even a few months past the end of fallback insurance can be really negative.

# Metrics to track
- NOT just GDP, market indexes, etc. especially in the case of AI-driven deflation. GDP is just a _projection_ of value onto dollars (or whatever unit of currency you prefer). It is not a perfect reflection (well known shortcomings like parental-provided childcare) and if AI removes lots of friction and obsoletes the "value add" industries built around that friction then those industries suffer and numbers go down (and unemployment etc.) BUT taking a broader non-financialized perspective, it's hard to argue that friction removal is "real value" lost. E.g. replacing horse/cart with cars, but if the cars were all free and managed naturally by the Earth. That's obviously not a net negative equal to value destroyed in horse/cart industry, even though since it's a natural resource/phenomenon numbers in GDP, stocks etc. don't go up.
- model miniaturization/distillation / how hardware needs scale with model capability: if you always need "10000 GPUs that each cost $50,000" to run a model, access can be centralized and all inputs/outputs monitored. If models can run locally, what is the capability or speed tradeoff? (not about absolutes/is it possible, but "will it take long enough to give us time to adapt")
- "I encourage economists to consider what metrics to monitor / new metrics to design in order to track AI disruption as it happens, so we're not left guessing and listening to theoretical discussions from random internet commenters." (see Philosophical musings section)

# Solution ideas (e.g. for the transition phase which could be extra painful) (beyond just personal/individual advice, which is interwoven into all parts of this essay)
- Ban _noncompetes_ nationally, or globally even, in case of _AI-caused layoffs_. It's important for the law to be extra clear, easy to apply, and inspiring confidence in let-go employees. Currently there is a mishmash of "sometimes this isn't enforceable" but you don't really know if you're safe from legal attacks and stress and this uncertainty itself is the real damage. There's a difference between a speed limit of 60, and a speed limit of 50 but "the police here are nice and never stop people who are going below 60." (I know this isn't viable in practice because judgement over "layoff cause" can easily be twisted ((at least not yet, but maybe some regulatory framework can be put in place to automatically categorize layoffs as AI-caused e.g. examining pre&post profit, if there was rehiriing, etc.)), but we're not entirely helpless here, e.g. unemployment schemes exist in many places and do provide some entry tests, like you don't get benefits for voluntarily leaving or for "gross misconduct".) This gives people opportunity to continue working in an area (different company, start a new company, etc.) that is best aligned with their expertise, not having to wait 12 or 18 months, which they'll need in an age of rapidly advancing, disruptive AI. On the other side this incentivizes well capitalized companies with lots of institutional knowledge to think more about how to retain workers (assigning them to new initiatives e.g.) rather than just letting them go for short term profit and stock price bumps.
- set up explicit, well-defined _guardrails for agentic AI_. Every single touchpoint where AI can affect the environment needs to be scrutinzed heavily, just like good public-facing API design. Predictability needs to be weighed against cost-of-failure. High costs of failure (e.g. anything that risks human life) must follow a standard where the AI access to tools or "effectuators" is no more harmful than a coherent or incoherent insane person with access to the same tools
- Don't use AI directly in sensitive scenarios; use (likely AI-generated) _artifacts, like scripts, which can be fully auditable_ (open-box / white-box) instead. This is basically a way to formalize/codify behavior in a _predictable_ way, with the help of AI, much like giving human employees a runbook so they know how to handle emergent situations that are nevertheless similar to ones that have shown up before. This is a relatively easy and super effective defense layer. (extend lunch ordering nuke launching example)
- government agencies like AISI (Britain) are already out there doing good work. We can give them real regulatory power (so coordinating with AI companies is not purely voluntary like now) and support - they are more connected to "promoting the general welfare" than the private sector. <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence_safety_institute>
- (perhaps more far-fetched) a price floor, i.e. tax like on alcohol/tobacco, on AI usage for "core operational routine cognitive tasks" by companies, exempting personal/non-profit use, R&D, etc. This is similar in intent to setting a minimum wage, though it will be more of an administrative burden. Should have a phaseout built in to avoid long-term dependence on "protectionism" and incentivize people to upskill in a non-distressed way. The idea is kind of like a tariff on work imported from the country of geniuses in a datacenter \*wink\*
