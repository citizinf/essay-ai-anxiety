# (I) An overview of AI anxiety

The early weeks of 2026 have been filled with intense public discussion about new and emerging risks developing as a result of powerful, rapidly advancing AI, especially in domains like national security and macroeconomic health. Many others have already written about these risks broadly and in detail—below I've shared a list of links to materials that I used in my research. Suffice it to say that the opportunity to upset the status quo, along with significant uncertainty about who the winners and losers will ultimately be, has people at all levels both excited and worried about the future.

Nowhere has this been on greater display than in worker and consumer confidence, which has been continuing its downward trend despite easing inflation, especially among higher-income earners. Well-publicized layoffs and increasing rates of long-term unemployment are now signs of a supposed "AI jobs apocalypse," and highly-educated people fear that AI will quickly solidify into a "general cognitive labor substitute," making it economically impossible to perform brainy work for a living ever again.

I'll go ahead and spoil the ending here by saying that I think this outcome is exceedingly unlikely, at least on a timescale that matters to people (we can use 40 years as a reference point for the average lifetime career). But I am by no means an AI skeptic, a viewpoint that becomes more untenable with each passing day. This technology is obviously transformational, and my optimism is based on the assumption that we, as a society, want desirable outcomes during and after the disruptions AI will bring, that we will be motivated to chart a clear path forward, and that we will be successful in following that path and course-correcting responsively.

I don't have a background in economics, nor do I have experience as an academic, entrepreneur, business leader, or professional investor. I also (luckily) don't have firsthand experience with layoffs, as I haven't been employed for years now (by choice—I decided to focus on other priorities, like raising my kids). I've been out of the labor market long enough that I've never personally seen how AI is used in a company's day-to-day operations. So what good can my viewpoint be? Well, before I quit working, I was a high-performing software engineer for six years, seven if you count a few summer internships. During my "sabbatical" I have continued to work on personal software projects, recently getting derailed by exploring AI coding tools, being wildly impressed, and falling into the rabbit hole of online essays and analysis. _The commentary so far has mostly been driven by intelligent laypeople making fuzzy, subjective, and sometimes sensationalistic claims; policy non-experts expressing opinions on policy; and technology non-experts expressing opinions on technology._ Obviously I'll be adding to this mismatch in some ways, but I think I can provide a fresh perspective as someone with serious professional technical expertise and a strong interest in economic policy and STS (my college minor). So far, at least, I haven't seen anything from someone who is both an engineer and not extremely rich from capital ownership (yet 😊). So here I am, being the change I want to see in the world.

Mr. Amodei has referred to AI development as a situation where "we can't stop the bus, but we can steer it" [^A14 [link](https://www.darioamodei.com/post/the-urgency-of-interpretability#:~:text=We%20can%E2%80%99t%20stop%20the%20bus)]. Building on this analogy, it's very important to recognize that _we do have some control over the process_, but we also need to know _how and why_ to exert that control. Just as it's critical to know where the steering wheel is, it's also critical to wipe the windshield and turn on the headlamps. All I'm trying to do here is make sure we can see where the bus is heading so we know when to actually turn the wheel.


# (III) The outcome that pessimists fear
A "dark AI" future is one filled with rampant threats to physical security and widespread economic subjugation. In this world, AI is broadly and completely powerful. Human cognition is inferior in all situations, and perfectly operating robots handle all manual labor. Control over critical pieces of AI infrastructure, like data centers, is centralized at the very top of society, and AI is only accessible to average people for trivial uses, or in ways that enhance societal control. Power struggles at the top and rogue-actor terrorism mean there are ever-present risks from cyberweapons, bioweapons, or worse. Average humans are so worthless that there's no official organization that prioritizes protecting individual lives. We have failed to overcome the three major challenges in a world without labor scarcity, as posed by Messrs. Autor and Thompson: life and community have no meaning; all resource allocation decisions are made unilaterally by the Altman-Musk-Zuckerberg commission, and all democratic governments have fallen and been replaced by technofeudalist states.

This really is the stuff of dystopian science-fiction nightmares. But perhaps that's why apocalyptic projections about AI have gone viral in the first place—dystopian science fiction is, for some reason, quite a popular genre of fiction. Just because something is widely explored in fiction doesn't mean it's destiny.

I do think that the dark AI outcome logically follows if all of these premises hold true: AI will advance far more rapidly than we can handle institutionally (destroying all white-collar jobs), AI-enhanced physical automation will mature shortly thereafter (destroying all blue-collar jobs), and we don't successfully design a just and sustainable system for resource distribution as the old capitalist wage-labor system inevitably breaks down. But we have some good reasons to expect that none of these premises are true, and many different strategies we can use to try to stave off their worst effects if they turn out to have a path to becoming reality.
