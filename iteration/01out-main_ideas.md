# Main ideas and paragraph map for `01in-essay_draft.md`

## Main ideas

### (I) Why is AI making people anxious?
- `MI01` — Public AI anxiety is being fueled by rapid progress, visible labor-market stress, and the fear that AI could become a general substitute for high-skill cognitive work.
- `MI02` — The essay takes that fear seriously but argues a total labor wipeout is unlikely on a career timescale, and it aims to offer a technically informed, research-backed view so society can steer AI wisely.

### (II) The outcome that pessimists fear
- `MI03` — The nightmare scenario is a fully AI-dominated society where both mental and manual labor are obsolete, power is centralized, and ordinary people lose security, meaning, and political power.
- `MI04` — That dystopia depends on several strong premises all coming true at once, and the essay argues those premises are contestable and open to intervention.

### (III) The physical world imposes limits
- `MI05` — AI feels weightless because users interact with it through screens, but its growth is constrained by scarce physical inputs such as land, electricity, chips, and capital.
- `MI06` — Even if models improve quickly, AI-driven progress in robotics, biotech, and other physical domains will move at the slower pace of real-world testing, manufacturing, and deployment.
- `MI07` — AI can make dangerous knowledge more accessible, but many serious threats still require hard-to-obtain materials and can be limited by regulation, screening, and defensive investment.

### (IV) Discovery is not advancement. We shouldn't extrapolate AI's abilities to infinity
- `MI08` — People habitually overread new technologies, so AI hype should not be confused with proof that capability will scale without bound.
- `MI09` — Scaling laws may describe how models extract finite patterns from human-created data, not a guaranteed march to arbitrary or superhuman intelligence.
- `MI10` — Popular AI benchmarks say much less than they seem to about real human-level performance because they often underweight reliability, coordination, and other hard-to-measure forms of competence.
- `MI11` — AI's genuine strengths are interface, speed, and flexible pattern handling, but many "magical" demos look less mystical once compared with older automation and ML techniques.

### (V) We stay in charge by integrating AI deliberately and avoiding technological determinist thinking
- `MI12` — AI is not an autonomous destiny machine; it is a human-deployed tool, which means design choices, governance, and regulation still shape its effects.
- `MI13` — The practical impact of AI depends not just on raw capability but on reliability and reviewability, since some tasks are easy to audit and others are not.
- `MI14` — Because AI remains unpredictable across a jagged frontier, high-stakes adoption should rely on constrained interfaces, interpretable artifacts, and durable human oversight.

### (VI) Consider how jobs can change, not how they can disappear
- `MI15` — Jobs are bundles of tasks, so AI can rebundle work instead of simply eliminating employment, sometimes even expanding demand for human labor.
- `MI16` — The central macroeconomic problem is preserving enough paid human work during the transition, because most people still need wages long before any true AI abundance arrives.
- `MI17` — Entry-level workers should be redesigned rather than discarded, though firms will need to overcome training, status, and office-politics barriers.
- `MI18` — To sustain employment, AI should be aimed at innovation and ambitious new projects that reveal new industries and new human tasks instead of just cutting costs.
- `MI19` — Deep technical expertise will remain valuable, with AI more likely to shorten the shelf life of specific hard skills than to make soft skills alone enough.

### (VII) The AI Revolution's transition problem
- `MI20` — Because AI might either reorganize labor markets or shrink them permanently, transition policy has to support people under multiple plausible end states.
- `MI21` — Governments need to counter the incentive to use AI mainly for cost-cutting, since synchronized layoffs can damage demand and only broader innovation can create new industries and jobs.
- `MI22` — Stronger competition can spread AI's gains more widely, reduce rent-extracting behavior, and lower barriers to new firm creation and experimentation.
- `MI23` — Public AI institutions and better prosperity metrics are necessary if society wants to measure AI's effects accurately instead of relying on stale signals like GDP or stock prices alone.
- `MI24` — Redistribution tools such as UBI must be built for a moving transition, not just for an imagined post-scarcity endpoint.
- `MI25` — AI ownership should be decentralized so a post-labor society does not leave most people dependent on a tiny class that controls abundance technologies.

### (VIII) A case for optimism
- `MI26` — The strongest reason for optimism is that AI disruption is likely to be slowed by physical bottlenecks, data limits, and oversight needs, buying society time to adapt.
- `MI27` — If society uses that time to redesign jobs, push innovation, protect competition, and diffuse key technologies, AI can create new wealth and curb concentration of power.
- `MI28` — Change is real, but it is likely gradual enough that policy and institutional choices can still help people keep stable livelihoods and share the benefits.
