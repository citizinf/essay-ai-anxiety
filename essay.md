# (I) An overview of AI anxiety

The early weeks of 2026 have been filled with intense public discussion about new and emerging risks developing as a result of powerful, rapidly advancing AI, especially in domains like national security and macroeconomic health. Many others have already written about these risks broadly and in detail—below I've shared a list of links to materials that I used in my research. Suffice it to say that the opportunity to upset the status quo, along with significant uncertainty about who the winners and losers will ultimately be, has people at all levels both excited and worried about the future.

Nowhere has this been on greater display than in worker and consumer confidence, which has been continuing its downward trend despite easing inflation, especially among higher-income earners. Well-publicized layoffs and increasing rates of long-term unemployment are now signs of a supposed "AI jobs apocalypse," and highly-educated people fear that AI will quickly solidify into a "general cognitive labor substitute," making it economically impossible to perform brainy work for a living ever again.

I'll spoil the ending here by saying that I think this outcome is exceedingly unlikely, at least on a timescale that matters (we can use 40 years as a reference point for the average lifetime career). But I am by no means an AI skeptic, a viewpoint that becomes more untenable with each passing day. This technology is obviously transformational, and my optimism is based on the assumption that we, as a society, want desirable outcomes during and after the disruptions AI will bring, and that we will be motivated enough to forge a clear path forward.

# (III) The outcome that pessimists fear

A "dark AI" future is one filled with rampant threats to physical security and widespread economic subjugation. In this world, AI is broadly and completely powerful. Human cognition is inferior in all situations, and perfectly operating robots handle all manual labor. Control over critical pieces of AI infrastructure, like data centers, is centralized at the very top of society, and AI is only accessible to average people for trivial uses, or in ways that enhance societal control. Power struggles at the top and rogue-actor terrorism mean there are ever-present risks from cyberweapons, bioweapons, or worse. Average humans are so worthless that there's no official organization that prioritizes protecting individual lives. We have failed to overcome the three major challenges in a world without labor scarcity, as posed by Messrs. Autor and Thompson: life and community have no meaning, all resource allocation decisions are made unilaterally by the Altman-Musk-Zuckerberg commission, and all democratic governments have fallen and been replaced by technofeudalist states.

This really is the stuff of dystopian science-fiction nightmares. But perhaps that's why apocalyptic projections about AI have gone viral in the first place—dystopian science fiction is, for some reason, quite a popular genre of fiction (emphasis on _fiction_). I do think that the dark AI outcome logically follows if all of these premises hold true: AI will advance far more rapidly than we can handle institutionally (destroying all white-collar jobs), AI-enhanced physical automation will mature shortly thereafter (destroying all blue-collar jobs), and we don't successfully design a just and sustainable system for resource distribution as the old capitalist wage-labor system inevitably breaks down. But we have good reasons to expect that perhaps none of these premises are true, and many different strategies we can use to try to stave off their worst effects if they turn out to have a path to becoming reality.


# (IV) The physical world imposes limits

The core user experience of AI is a digital, "disembodied" one. Sure, you need to use some kind of computer interface, but those are easily exchanged and replaced—nothing happens to the AI if you lose your phone or laptop. Because the actual physical machinery of AI is hidden by the internet (and all its wireless connections and miles of buried or undersea cables), it's easy to misunderstand what AI actually needs to work, much like how someone who played The Sims too much can be astonished at how complicated it is to build a real house—it can take 18+ months even if you're infinitely rich!

Land, electricity, GPUs, and GPU components (like memory chips) are all indispensible inputs for AI, and right now they are all in short supply (yes, including land, which is available only through the goodwill of local communities). Any new production is capital-intensive and will take time to ramp up. This is why people are seriously floating the idea of putting data centers in space. Clearly this is an obstacle to AI everything, everywhere, all at once. Consumer-facing prices still look reasonable because, just as in the early days of Uber, they are heavily subsidized by investors. Masa is willing to burn even more on AI than the (inflation-adjusted) $100+ billion he lost during the dot-com crash, and that's one guy.

Outside of the economic costs of simply expanding the functionality we already have, using AI to press ahead with innovation in important physical domains like robotics and biotech will be far slower than progress in the AI models themselves (a purely digital endeavor). The physical world always runs at 1x speed, which drags out testing and iteration cycles. Even Mr. Amodei has written that AI may be "limited by the response time of the physical world or of software it interacts with." Providing some concrete examples will really drive this key point home. If AI has produced genetically modified crop seeds, we still have to wait for them to grow just like any other plant. Robots have to be manufactured and assembled before we can test how well their AI software works. Few people would be willing to take a new AI-designed pill before animal trials have been run. AI absolutely can accelerate progress in these fields, but not to the point where a single small team can drop new finalized discoveries every month like AI labs releasing new model versions. I am reminded of a classic maxim often repeated in the software industry: nine women can't make a baby in one month.

Regarding security, AI will increase the accessibility of engaging in terrorism and other nefarious activity. But being able to successfully execute a terrorist attack isn't just a knowledge problem; it's also a physical one. Let's say I want to build a nuke. I can't just ask ChatGPT to deliver a pile of uranium to my front door, no matter how well I understand the physics of bomb-building. Sure, it's a ridiculous example, but it illustrates a reasonable way to approach the issue. What if I wanted nitrogen fertilizer instead? As with uranium, the government exerts some control over that [^B1 [link](https://chatgpt.com/share/69a51084-314c-8013-a96a-2a160a7c7d74#:~:text=strict%20security%20oversight%20on%20ammonium%2Dnitrate%20fertilizer)]. Maybe this is the type of "heavy-handed" economic disruption that Mr. Amodei wants to avoid, but society seems to accept it. The need is clearly understood, though loss of life was sadly required to get us there.

One of the reasons why those who worry about AI safety focus so much on bioterrorism and cybersecurity is that these attack strategies have minimal physical material requirements. But even here, I think fortune favors the good guys, if we can adopt the right defenses. Restrictions similar to those applied to fertilizer can also be applied to biotech inputs, like centrifuges which are readily available on Amazon, or gene synthesis companies which are happy to print out dangerous biological agents [^A15 [link](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1hNUnU8i2yubt5uesmmV17aTJXhYYDgTY)]. Yes it's unpleasant, but remember COVID? As for cyberattacks, Mr. Amodei has already made the key observation: the offense-defense profile is significantly more balanced, and we'll be in good shape as long as we provide generous support (including AI) to white-hat and purple-teaming cybersecurity researchers.


# (V) Discovery is not advancement. We shouldn't extrapolate AI's abilities to infinity

New technology has always been accompanied by optimism about its potential—optimism that often ends up looking hopelessly naive in hindsight. Bitcoin, or other blockchain tokens more generally, was supposed to help solve world hunger, poverty, and corruption [^B2 [link](https://chatgpt.com/share/69a63890-6928-8013-b1bc-cf99eaa1c640#:~:text=narrative%20held%20that%20blockchain%20technology%20could%20%E2%80%9Csolve%E2%80%9D%20structural%20global%20problems)]. We are well past 50 years overdue for "real" self-driving cars, based on the earliest predictions for when they would be available [^B3 [link](https://chatgpt.com/share/69a63871-6538-8013-b639-13a69aa071d3#:~:text=predictions%20about%20widespread%20self%2Ddriving%20cars%20appeared%20surprisingly%20early)]. This pattern extends beyond technology; for example, many of the earliest uses for crude oil were as misguided medicinal treatments for skin, respiratory, and digestive issues [^B4 [link](https://chatgpt.com/share/69a640f7-2cf0-8013-9dea-ea3f23ec2d13#:~:text=petroleum%20products%20that%20were%20widely%20promoted%20at%20the%20time%20but%20later%20proved%20exaggerated%2C%20incorrect%2C%20or%20outright%20harmful)]. I think OpenAI's decision to present their LLM research as a chatbot was a stroke of product genius, because chatbots have enthralled people since the early days of ELIZA despite obvious limitations [^A16 [link](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ELIZA_effect)]. Remove those obvious limitations and you get the fastest-growing consumer application in history, and the wild claims to match.

One big pillar supporting the belief in AI's unstoppable path to world domination is AI's "scaling laws," often illustrated with a now-famous METR graph drawing an exponentially increasing curve [^A17 [link](https://metr.org/blog/2025-03-19-measuring-ai-ability-to-complete-long-tasks)]. (The graph is usually shown as a straight line but with a logarithmic y-axis.) As The New Yorker's recent profile of Anthropic [^A18 [link](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2026/02/16/what-is-claude-anthropic-doesnt-know-either)] tells it, Mr. Amodei and some close colleagues were important players in prioritizing model size (input and parameter count) over "fancy contraptions," and proven right when simply "scaling" size well past what other teams thought reasonable led to something robust enough to eventually power ChatGPT. That might explain why, on a psychological level, he still places such faith in scaling, saying he has "increasing confidence in the trajectory of AI and the likelihood that it will surpass human ability across the board."

LLM-based AI is quite unlike past technologies, however. We're "growing" these models rather than designing them. What if AI's improving capabilities are being "found"—discovered—rather than created? A claim like "more hardware and inputs and parameters means more model capability" could map to something like "more pumpjacks and wells means more oil." The knowledge, information, and connections are all already there in the training data, generated by humans with human limitations, and the training process "extracts" a model, much like how all the oil is already there in an oil field, and the extraction hardware pulls it out. Adding more hardware at the margins can improve extraction, but it doesn't inevitably follow that the total amount extracted will reach infinity or some arbitrarily high value—the limit is how much was originally there in the first place, and scaling laws don't say anything about that. They are based only on statistical patterns derived from what we've seen so far [^B5 [link](https://chatgpt.com/share/69a637ac-2bd8-8013-9ec5-e7a175be3252#:~:text=What%20Scaling%20Laws%20Do%20Not%20Say)].

Consider this thought experiment: if we only train LLMs with material created by average 5-year-olds, even if we can scale infinitely, will we ever see adult-level reasoning or output quality? Probably not, and the same logic can be extended to superhuman outputs falling out of human inputs.

Now let's take a look at what's actually being measured by the people testing AI systems. Closely examine the METR graph, and you'll notice the y-axis measures time for a 50% chance of succeeding. A human employee who successfully completes tasks 50% of the time would be fired real quick. But ok, that's an unfair critique, I agree. They have an 80% graph as well. My point is that the graph _implies something_ about AI's growing abilities, but _it's not clear how that something actually relates to human ability more generally_. The details matter a lot here, because they determine what AI's limits actually are, whether AI will enhance or replace jobs, and how AI tools need to be used and audited. Maybe a 99% graph would be more useful, but would it have the same shape? Collaboration and integration between many teams is a core part of most human work, but how well can AI coordinate between agents? Regarding AI's top-tier performance on standardized tests, wouldn't it be more fair to compare that against the performance of a human with internet access? (Or, if you believe overfitting on the training data is a problem, a human with the answer key?) And how good can AI actually get at prompting itself, a likely prerequisite to becoming fully autonomous [^B6 [link](https://chatgpt.com/share/69a6d5ee-76bc-8013-809e-b173aa4d69cd#:~:text=will%20AI%20learn%20problem%2Dframing%20itself%3F)]? The graph says nothing about any of this. Also, much of this stuff is difficult to benchmark in an objective, numerical way, making it hard to measure AI performance in these areas and train it to get better.

AI is advancing, no doubt. But not all forms of advancement lead to becoming "better" than humans. An AI that can only produce 90%-of-the-time-reliable work while getting faster and faster to the point that it is almost instantaneous is still not going to be better than a human in a problem space where any adequate speed is acceptable but high reliability is critical.

I think AI's revolutionary, general-purpose power boils down to just a few fundamental components after stripping away all of the ornamentation and plugins: human-like natural-language interface and processing, speed, and clever mapping of a "token prediction" process to non-linguistic domains like images and audio. Beyond just text processing, NLP vastly increases ease of use (no more learning 50 buttons and 100 drop-down menu items, or a whole programming language, before you can do anything useful) and customizability (vibe coding). Speed has obvious impacts on R&D and productivity. And any artifact that can be reduced to a structured stream of data can be ingested, reproduced, and refined. These building blocks will unlock vast opportunities as we begin to learn how to use them. But we also need to avoid getting carried away attributing more to AI's ability than it deserves.

In his article, Mr. Shumer expresses amazement at AI that not only codes up an app, but that also "opens the app itself, clicks through the buttons, tests the features, uses the app the way a person would." I'm sure watching it work is mesmerizing, but so is watching Selenium, a browser automation library available since 2004, run through a Selenese script. Similar tools for testing mobile apps have also been around for a long time. Another example that comes to mind is when a colleague asked me to try out a ChatGPT image recognition feature. Attach a surreal image, like a man pressing shirts on an ironing board while sitting on his car trunk on a busy city street, and then ask, "what's weird about this picture?" You'll probably get a sensible answer, but I can also give you a sensible answer without using ChatGPT (though not in natural language). I'd take some open-source image recognition library and get a list of all objects in the scene, then train a basic ML model on a bunch of image keyword sets from Getty Images, then run the list against the model to determine which are the odd ones out. #city #street #cars is likely a common grouping in Getty Images; #city #cars #ironing_board is likely not. ML techniques like this simplified example predate ChatGPT by decades.

I am _not_ saying that modern AI is using these "primitive" techniques under the hood—I'm sure that today's models have built their own original, inscrutable procedures for handling such problems somewhere in their billions and billions of parameters. I am pointing out that we need to keep AI accomplishments in perspective, by comparing them to what was already possible before AI. Seeing AI's achievements in sharp detail, rather than as broad, blinding superiority in every imaginable way, will help us integrate AI into society in a sane, prudent, and minimally disruptive way.


# (VI) We stay in charge by integrating AI deliberately and avoiding technological determinist thinking

Technological determinism is a school of thought that treats technology as an independent phenomenon exerting a one-way force on society and culture. A recent example of this thinking style is "smartphones and social media are worsening teen loneliness and anxiety." A popular one from last century is "TV damaged intellectual and civic culture by replacing reading and socializing with passive entertainment." These simplified claims are not _wrong_ per se, but they overlook an assortment of other non-technological factors that might explain those ill effects. Smartphones are not like alien invaders shooting radio-wave depression bombs into teenagers' heads. They are designed by people, and the apps that they run are also designed by people, and if there is some problem with those apps, we need to understand why people designed them the way they did. Humans still retain a lot of agency (and responsibility).

Language itself can cause us to slip into this mode of thinking. After hearing enough "AI will take your job," "AI will increase inequality," "AI will make business software obsolete," you might be forgiven for thinking of AI as an evil mastermind plotting chaos and destruction from an underground bunker beneath his private island. A _tool_ is what AI actually is, and like any other tool, a human needs to wield it for it to do any good. There are two main takeaways from this observation. First, a _human_ is doing the wielding. AI is a very fancy tool that can spin up its own subtools, but barring any killer robot uprising, there will always be a person at the top level—so ultimately people will be needed, in charge, and responsible. Second, just as a hammer can be used to build a house or bash someone's skull in, and nuclear fission can be used to generate electricity or vaporize 100,000 people, the consequences greatly depend on _how we choose to use AI_. Choices can be influenced by the legal environment, so governments and regulation will, unsurprisingly, play a crucial role in promoting wise usage of AI.

Two key properties will inform how we integrate AI: _reliability_ and _reviewability_. These are intertwined but distinct concepts. Reliability is well-understood as something important to watch; the public lexicon has already incorporated a new sense of the word "hallucination," essentially referring to an unpredictable instance of AI producing unreliable (i.e. false or made-up) output. Reviewability, on the other hand, is quite underappreciated. It refers to variation in how efficiently someone can look over outputs and results for different tasks. While AI vastly lowers the human effort needed to _generate_ things, effort needed to _audit_ things stays mostly the same, unless you are confident enough to let AI handle that as well. Many skills that have become early casualties of AI are ones that produce artifacts with very high reviewability, like image generation (anyone can look at an image for a couple seconds and answer the question, "is this good enough?") and app prototyping (you can just try the app to see if it works).

But some tasks are intrinsically less reviewable. Let's say you want to summarize a long document, but not casually—suppose it's critically important that you capture every main idea in the original, and avoid any made-up points that can't be mapped back to something in the original. So you start by asking AI for a summary, but notice that because AI _reliability_ is not 100%, you _must_ read the entire original to ensure nothing was missed, and you _must_ read the entire summary to ensure nothing was made up. It's still more efficient than doing it without AI, where you'd read the entire original and _write_ the entire summary, because reading is faster than writing. But this task's _reviewability_ profile caps the maximum theoretical efficiency gain from using AI. Replace "summarize a document" with "review security camera footage," and now there's no difference between "reading" and "writing" (they both map to "watch"), and consequently no efficiency gain from using AI.

Perhaps only a small minority of human activity actually has reliability requirements greater than what AI can achieve, and a cost of failure high enough to necessitate thorough and exhaustive reviews. A casual summary with potential minor errors is probably good enough in most cases. Even so, we will clearly need humans to make important decisions about whether AI is reliable enough to replace humans or not, and to derive reasonable productivity-multiplier expectations based on how reviewable AI outputs are. This is not just an abstract concern. If we get it wrong, we can turn people into what Mr. Doctorow calls "reverse centaurs"—humans who are not enhanced by AI (regular centaurs), but whose real purpose is to be used by AI as an accountability sink, "not really overseeing the AI's work, but taking the blame for the AI's mistakes."

Scenarios which are highly sensitive and/or have a high cost of failure will require deliberate and creative human oversight. Consider this contrived example: A country's military headquarters runs computers that can launch nukes, as well as computers that employees can use to order lunch. A new AI agent has just been installed on-premises. Every day it has a 99% chance of ordering everyone the most optimal lunch based on voice commands, preferences, and current prices. It also has a 1% chance of accessing the nuke control computers and launching nukes. Should this AI be allowed to continue operating? Of course not—but this setup is analogous to other, more realistic scenarios, where competitive pressures and costs of failure that are not so comically high will induce organizations to keep these AI systems around. Maybe it's an AI that has write access to databases, or one that may randomly choose to ignore items on the safety checklist (we don't know how they work, so we can't expect them to always follow instructions).

Mitigating these problems will require human involvement. Rather than giving an unpredictable, black-box AI direct access to critical systems, we can use (likely AI-generated, but human-reviewed) _artifacts, like scripts, with fully understandable inner workings_. This formalizes behavior into something completely predictable, much like giving human employees a runbook so they don't have to wing it in emergent situations. The tradeoff is an extra layer that increases friction by preventing "live" AI access, but that often won't be as important as having an easy and very effective defensive layer. A basic example of this can even be found in OpenAI's documentation: an "auth-translation sidecar" which hides passwords from an AI model by using placeholders instead, only injecting the real password into requests that the model has already sent out. This prevents the model from "learning" the password and then accidentally revealing it elsewhere. This sort of arrangement could describe the next phase of the software industry as a whole—it distinguishes agents which use other software tools from agents which try to handle all the details themselves.

I think that a conservative way to reason about this generally is to limit all touchpoints a live, running model can use to produce side effects, in such a way that an insane person with access to the same touchpoints is unable to do any real damage beyond making a mess. Rules like "read-only default with a small write sandbox" and "ask for confirmation first" are widely applicable, but the specifics of a particular situation also need to be considered.

The unpredictability of AI is one of the major aspects of what is often called its "jagged frontier," the idea that there are some tasks where it clearly outperforms humans and some where it clearly doesn't. Early adopters of enterprise AI have seen weird errors that just don't happen with human common sense, including overproduction of holiday limited-edition CPGs as a response to manufacturing issues, and overly liberal distribution of refunds [^A19 [link](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/03/01/ai-artificial-intelligence-economy-business-risks.html)]. Some maximalist predictions assume this is just a brief stage before AI outperforms humans in every imaginable way, but if the jagged frontier era turns out to last longer (we could expect decades, given everything I've discussed above), that gives us ample time to adjust economically and institutionally, and it gives humans something to do: identifying AI's gaps and filling them in. As the jagged frontier evolves, so will the strategies we use to integrate AI in a reasonable way, so I think we still have quite a lot of work ahead of us.

There's plenty more to say about safety, security, and military use of AI for R&D and operations, but I don't have any additional ideas worth including here. I'll set that topic aside for the remainder of the essay and focus solely on economic consequences of AI and the future of work.


# (VII) Consider how jobs can change, not how they can disappear

A job is just a bundle of tasks and responsibilities, and job boundaries are flexible. It may be the case that a particular set of tasks done by a single person in 2025 will no longer support a standalone job in 2030, but that does not condemn people in 2030 to have to fight for fewer job openings than existed in 2025. There are several factors at play which can support or increase the number of available jobs. A commonly cited example is bank tellers, a job which had several core tasks automated when the ATM was introduced, but also saw employment increase afterwards as demand for more complex banking services grew and banks opened new branches. Messrs. Autor and Thompson discuss how accountants and inventory clerks were affected differently by computerization—accountants had their simpler tasks automated, increasing the expertise required for remaining and new tasks, and increasing wages while reducing total employment, while the mirror image was true of inventory clerks, who had their more complex tasks automated, decreasing required expertise and wages, but increasing total employment as newly qualified workers entered the labor pool.

Maybe these examples are too small in scope, though. The fear with AI is that, as a general-purpose technology, it can cause a productivity shock that hits everyone at about the same time, greatly reducing aggregate demand for "human work hours" and leaving few places for the slack to go. I think it's important to analyze AI's economic impact broadly instead of extrapolating from specific cases. So, taking a system-wide view, the problem can be formulated as follows: we need to find a way to support demand for roughly the same total human work hours (or the same proportion of human work hours to labor market size, taking population size into account). AI can perform many tasks at a higher speed and lower cost than humans, putting downward pressure on demand for human work hours. How do we stimulate demand and maximize human hours used for employment?

Before we get into the details, it's worth noting that this sounds like a rather unenviable goal. Didn't Keynes imagine that we might one day have machines that make us so productive, work would become optional? (The famous "15 hours a week" prediction was actually a compromise to support humanity's presumed _spiritual_ need for work in order to not drain life of all meaning and purpose.) Why should we desperately try to maintain the 40-hour workweek now that the AI machine, something that really might have the potential to end scarcity altogether, has finally arrived? Of course, we already know the answer: anxiety around job security due to AI's uneven impacts. If AI takes my job and makes me unemployable, how will I pay rent? How will I afford to eat? The problem with overly optimistic thinking about the future "age of AI abundance" is that it fails to distinguish between two different types of joblessness: someone who _doesn't require a job_ to get everything they need (or want), and someone who _depends on job income_ to meet their needs. AI labor displacement is already underway, and it is glaringly obvious that most people who can't find a job fall into the second group, which exists because AI abundance _is not here yet_.

I'll explore uncertainty about the future of workers in an AI-powered economy in the next section. Right now, I'll focus on ways we can theoretically reallocate human work hours to maintain employment, which is an accepted and politically stable way of receiving money. Having work available will ease any disruption for people who become early casualties of AI automation, buying us time to design better policy for the moment (if it ever arrives) when permanent job loss becomes inevitable.

One macroeconomic, economy-wide segment of the labor pool that has received much attention is entry-level workers. Mr. Amodei went on the public record in 2025 to predict that up to half of all entry-level white-collar jobs could be displaced in just a few years. However, saying that entry-level work is at risk can conflate entry-level _people_ and entry-level _positions_. An entry-level person is someone who doesn't have much work experience when starting at an organization or company. An entry-level position is a specific bundle of tasks. These particular task bundles may no longer be available on the job market in five years (the same could be said of many other jobs too, not just entry-level ones). So why not just give entry-level people different task bundles? Could a company refashion its entry-level bundles to be, say, 20% oversight of AI work that used to be done by people, and 80% human-centric tasks which AI has not yet successfully automated?

This thought experiment reveals a few assumptions and unwritten societal expectations about work, many of which may need to be revamped in short order. Some people claim that the higher-level human tasks are too hard for an inexperienced newbie to handle. But that is by no means a showstopper—consider how apprenticeships work in the trades, or how residency works in medicine. Everyone has to start from somewhere. Maybe entry-level workers are a net tax, demanding salaries while generating relatively little value for the firm. That is shortsighted thinking, an indication of vastly inefficient use of human capital. The entry-level worker pool offers large, consistent supply (new grads every year), people willing to work for lower pay, and less recruiting overhead for employers (college campuses provide centralization and networks which make hiring far more efficient than putting postings up online and sifting through tons of resumes). Give it a couple more years, and today's students are likely to have received a formal, structured education on AI's use cases, strengths, and weaknesses, compared to existing workers who may be trying to figure things out piecemeal on an outdated educational foundation. There may be a psychological barrier that experienced workers have to overcome in order to maximize the potential of AI, since they can view it as a threat to their livelihoods; entry-level workers have nothing to lose. And entry-level workers are on average more youthful, which corresponds to higher ambition, greater AI-native familiarity, and more fluid intelligence—all essential characteristics for dealing with fast-moving, disruptive technologies.

Office politics may be one of the biggest obstacles faced by entry-level workers in this new era. The nastier aspects of human nature are unavoidable in the workplace, and experienced workers will doubtless be loath to train fresh faces on tasks that they themselves didn't have an opportunity to perform before logging many years of service. New entry-levels, along with AI, could be seen as yet another front in the battle to defend one's livelihood, whether that be prevailing salaries or the job itself. This dynamic already exists in rules such as "you need to work here for two years before you can get promoted." It could be much exacerbated by making unwilling mentors out of people who had to stay up late, skip vacation, deal with toxic bosses, chew up their personal relationships, freeze their eggs, miss their kids' school plays, and whatever else it may be, just to get the chance to perform work high up on the corporate ladder. Surely they will be unhappy seeing the next generation get to the same "level" with fewer sacrifices. If proficiency with office politics becomes more entrenched as a determinant of success, that will favor experienced workers, but I don't see that as a form of expertise that benefits society more broadly. Hopefully, at least something about workplace culture can change to ensure entry-level people get the support they need.

It is completely reasonable for experienced workers to want to protect their livelihoods. So let's look beyond entry-level challenges, and try to see how we can support human work at all experience levels. If we direct more energy towards innovation, and less to efficiency or cost-cutting, we have a much better chance of creating totally new products, systems, or industries, all of which come with new attendant career opportunities. AI makes possible things that previously were "obviously" impossible, so we should rethink our assumptions about which innovative "moonshots" are remotely feasible and worth investigating. We can tease out human work from AI work with an approach along the following lines. Start with a realistic "driver" AI, a "CEO" that can spin up its own AI subordinates or physical robots, while only possessing the capabilities of models from today or the near future. Next, imagine prompting this AI with a crazy moonshot idea. Maybe something like, "You have access to a bunch of factories, robots, raw materials, electricity, and rockets. Design an inhabitable lunar base that can support 50,000 people and build it on the Moon." (An actual moonshot.) Or, "Design a series of nanobots which can be injected into any cancer patient's bloodstream in order to suppress or kill malignant tumors, and start manufacturing them." Or, "Solve world hunger (killing everyone because dead people aren't hungry doesn't count)." Then, imagine all of the random crap that doesn't work (if you draw a blank here, you're welcome to actually try the prompt). Imagine fixing the first layer of problems, then iterating with a re-prompt, then fixing the next layer of problems. And there it is—you're thinking about human work opportunities right now!

Some of these ideas can become self-sustaining businesses, while others may need investor or government support. This isn't really any different from how capitalism already works. In all these cases, we'll get support for existing jobs and brand-new ones, including in areas beyond the "AI oversight" family like the ones in this NYT Magazine article [^A20 [link](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/06/17/magazine/ai-new-jobs.html)].

I don't want to discourage people by only bringing up breathtaking ideas of world-changing scope. While many of the more popularized ideas do try to be wildly ambitious, like those being tackled by Google's "Moonshot Factory" [^A21 [link](https://x.company/projects)], there are all sorts of dimensions that can be tweaked to make a project moonshot-like. Increasing speed is one (shorten the length of process X from weeks to days), as long as the benefits of a successfully completed project are used to expand a growing company (or spin off a new one) rather than cut costs. People could also focus on technical achievements (build a thing to solve this hard problem that actually works), matters of human taste (make an extremely effective marketing campaign), and other complementary goals like identifying challenges (how can our existing processes and pain points be improved) and success metrics (what actually needs to be measured, and how, to judge if we "eradicated world poverty"). The point is to focus on doing _new things_ rather than downsizing on _old things_, and thinking about the gaps when prompting AI can distill out real human tasks so that the new things don't also end up getting eaten by AI.

Some commentators have mused about soft skills superseding hard skills as people shift to making "uniquely human" contributions that AI supposedly cannot reproduce. This strikes me as misguided. Of course developing skills that we can perform better than AI is important, but we aren't close to achieving the moonshot goals from above just with communication, collaboration, and things of that nature. Deep technical human expertise is still going to be necessary and valuable, maybe for generations. Most likely, AI's main impact here will be to shorten the shelf life of particular skills as it quickly learns them itself, meaning that hard-skill-oriented workers will find they must continue learning and upskilling throughout their careers. This should not be too daunting because of how familiar it feels, at least in domains like software development where continually learning new frameworks, languages, and techniques is just part of the daily job description.


# (VIII) A mere technological leap, or the arrival of post-scarcity and permanent end of wage labor? We have to deal with a "transition problem" either way

In the previous section, I described the _possibility_ of restructuring work to focus on growth and innovation. But why would free actors in liberal economies _choose_ this path instead of just cutting costs and boosting efficiency? If our best policies and incentives fall short of maintaining full employment, what are we going to do about the people who find themselves out of work?

AI might take us into an age of post-scarcity, or it might only evolve wage-labor capitalism while keeping it intact. Either way, the labor market will be destabilized, and people will need financial support, whether for reskilling or permanently. The transitional period could come with significant political, social, and economic upheaval, and because the path AI will take is uncertain, we can't yet confidently predict what the end state will be. We need solution ideas to handle both possibilities—one where the labor market reorganizes but eventually achieves full employment again, and the other where the labor market shrinks permanently or becomes extinct. Luckily, many approaches will likely be helpful in either case. Post-scarcity won't arrive overnight, and it's not futile to prop up the labor market even if it ultimately disappears, because it will be a financial lifeline for people during the transition, as we figure out the right systems and institutional design for distributing wealth in a world without work.

There's already a well-established academic tradition of studying employment shocks, like the Industrial Revolution and globalization, and finding evidence-backed solutions to support affected workers. So the first obvious solution idea is to "use the solutions we already have." This covers well-known programs like unemployment insurance, welfare transfers, and public housing, as well as other ideas posed by serious, professional economists. Here are some additional ideas I find compelling:

**(1) Get the government involved in discouraging AI cost-cutting.** The rationale for prioritizing cost-cutting and margin-fattening can be briefly summarized as follows: stocks go up. Key decision-makers are basically always shareholders, and naturally they will prefer to make choices that enrich themselves. This is a perennial bugbear for people of a certain political persuasion, but it has historically been associated with widespread prosperity, and is usually not considered to be a systemic problem, even when the companies performing layoffs are already profitable. The reason why AI really could be different is that it cuts a clear path for _many companies_, across _many industries_, all at _the same time_, to automate away any white-collar work that is feasible to automate. Shrinking payroll (while maintaining output) directly boosts margins and the stock price, while investing in R&D has slower and less obvious payoffs. Each company is thus making a rational decision, but the sheer scale and pace of the activity makes it a new kind of risk.

I really appreciated Citrini bringing up the circular flow model in their research note, because it succinctly illustrates the problem. The economy "works" by transferring money in one direction and value in the other direction. Any participant that suddenly has less money will spend less and slow the circular flow. The US economy is 70% driven by consumer spending, and those consumers are exactly the ones who will have less money if they lose their incomes. It is obvious that if _a lot_ of consumers lose their incomes _all at once_, that will slow the economy, and while the outcome may not be as extreme as Citrini's "doom loop" scenario, it certainly could show up as a recession, and quite an ugly one if the number of affected consumers is relatively large. This core observation about the size and rate of job losses is one that many techno-optimists seem to have totally missed. Yes, cutting costs improves the bottom line, but _everybody cutting costs simultaneously is also going to hit top-line revenue_, and then it's not clear if your bottom line will improve or suffer.

Skeptics often cite the Industrial Revolution as evidence that new technology may require painful adjustments but won't cause widespread permanent unemployment. Counting on this analogy to save us is dangerously complacent (the Industrial Revolution was demographically so different, occuring in an era of population growth and economic integration of former subsistence farmers, that it might actually _support_ the argument that AI will kill jobs [^B7 [link](https://chatgpt.com/share/69b15305-7484-8013-b5de-0965768dbade#:~:text=Why%20the%20analogy%20to%20AI%20may%20fail)]). Jobs are indeed being automated away, but that's not what leads to new industries and job creation. The nature of the technology itself, what it makes newly possible in the physical world, is what produces new industries. Agriculture reshaped population size, military defense, and bureaucracy; industry gave us mass production, steel, and oil; information technology gave rise to cyberspace and basically wiped out any sense of communications latency. AI's purely digital form makes it excellent at reducing the cost of digital work, but there doesn't seem to be an obvious path for "pure AI" to create whole new lines of work beyond AI oversight positions. An AI revolution also has to spawn a robotics revolution, manufacturing revolution, and so on, in order to create jobs—and that means innovation.

All this isn't really a "solution" so much as a rationale for why we will need the government to be heavily involved. We are faced with a collective action problem, like constrained housing supply or climate change, where everyone agrees that a situation is bad, but the most rational ways an individual actor can behave just make the problem worse.

**(2) Incentivize vigorous competition over monopolistic success.**

**(3) Design new metrics for measuring wealth and well-being.** deflation, GDP down, stocks down, due to "free" AI or competed-down margins... Altman deflation isn't bad if not too uneven... (due to labor devaluation, but "actual wealth" is going up, making these metrics misleading and in need of an overhaul - examples like parental childcare; water freely available from Earth instead of needing extraction; doing DIY home improvement instead of hiring labor) CITRINI circular flow stagnation (Citrini: cost-cutting will not make all firms more profitable because they will also lose revenue with a big enough white-collar employment hit! This is a core observation which a lot of techno-optimists seem to be totally missing.)

**(4) Support governmental AI institutions.** uneven casualties meaning some need early support - how to detect this (AISI "future of work" metric monitoring?)

**(5) Get serious about designing viable wealth and resource redistribution systems.** "ideas like UBI or capital ownership are great for the end state, but the transition will be the real pain" ... this is necessary but not sufficient...

**(6) Decentralize AI.**
